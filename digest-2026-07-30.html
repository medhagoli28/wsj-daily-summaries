Meta’s Case for Its AI Spending Keeps Getting Weaker
Meta reported Q2 2026 results on July 29 that showed revenue up 28% to roughly $60.8 billion but earnings per share of $6.18, missing consensus by about 14% after a $2.4 billion legal charge and severance costs. Capital expenditures hit $31.08 billion in the quarter, up 83% from $17.01 billion a year earlier, and the company narrowed full-year 2026 capex guidance to $130–$145 billion versus $72.2 billion spent in 2025. The clearest signal of strain was free cash flow, which collapsed to $784 million from $8.55 billion a year earlier even as operating cash flow rose 25% to $31.86 billion. Shares fell roughly 8% on the session and close to 10% after hours, with Luke Stillman, managing director at research firm Madison and Wall, saying "Meta is investing everything into AI capacity, and that's what has investors spooked every time earnings are announced." Unlike Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet, Meta disclosed no signed customer backlog to justify the buildout, though Mark Zuckerberg floated renting out compute and a "large enterprise opportunity" spanning agents, APIs and internal software.

Johnson & Johnson Comes to Strategic Agreement with Sail Biomedicines
On July 29, 2026, Johnson & Johnson announced a collaboration with Flagship Pioneering-backed Sail Biomedicines to advance in vivo CAR-T therapies aimed at "immune reset" in autoimmune and immune-mediated diseases. J&J is making total initial payments of $785 million, including a $465 million equity investment, plus up to $140 million in contingent development milestone payments. The deal grants J&J an exclusive option to acquire Sail outright for $2.58 billion, bringing potential total value to roughly $925 million before the buyout option or about $3.5 billion including it. J&J said that, assuming it exercises the option, the agreements would dilute adjusted operational EPS and adjusted EPS by approximately $0.18 in 2026 and about $1.28 in 2027. The announcement was disclosed in an 8-K filed the same day, alongside J&J's completed acquisition of Firefly Bio.

Samsung Sees Chip Crunch Through 2028 as Profit Soars to Record
Samsung Electronics reported Q2 2026 results on July 30 with all-time-high consolidated revenue of 171.5 trillion won (up 28% quarter-on-quarter) and record operating profit of 89.5 trillion won, roughly $62 billion — a 1,814% jump year over year and up 56% sequentially, driven by record DRAM and NAND sales into AI servers. Jaejune Kim, executive vice president of Samsung's memory business, told analysts that "the supply shortage in 2027 is expected to worsen compared to this year, and it is expected to continue in 2028," citing delayed ramp-ups of new DRAM lines that won't add meaningful output until the second half of 2027. Samsung said it has signed long-term supply agreements — at least five years each — with the top five global data-center firms and is close to deals with five more, together covering 60% to 70% of its total capacity. The memory boom cut the other way internally: the mobile and networks unit posted 33.2 trillion won of revenue but swung to a 700 billion won operating loss as component costs surged. Despite the record, shares fell about 7% as investors weighed AI capex and Chinese competition.

Mark Zuckerberg Says U.S. Should Accelerate AI Development, Not Restrict It
In an opinion column published Tuesday, July 28, 2026, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg argued the U.S. should speed up domestic AI development rather than constrain it, saying the benefits of distributing AI broadly outweigh the risks "by quite a margin." He pushed back on what he called an overly pessimistic public mood, saying "we have a lot of data points at this point, and that should point us to be much more optimistic than I believe the current discourse reflects." Zuckerberg specifically criticized the Trump administration's executive order establishing a voluntary 30-day pre-release review period for new AI models, noting that in a field moving this fast "that actually is quite a meaningful amount of time." He also opposed proposals to bar Chinese open-weight models from the U.S. market, arguing a ban wouldn't achieve its intended effect and would cede ground rather than protect it. The timing sharpened an industry split: the same week, OpenAI and Anthropic endorsed a petition from 1,224 frontier-lab employees urging governments to build technical and governance tools to slow AI if systems begin self-improving faster than humans can supervise.

A Backlash Against Anthropic Is Brewing in Silicon Valley
Anthropic, the world's most valuable startup, became the only frontier lab to refuse to sign an open letter led by Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang urging Washington not to restrict open-weight AI models, after Google and OpenAI both signed over the weekend of July 25–26, 2026. CEO Dario Amodei published a blog post on Monday, July 27 insisting "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," and instead proposed three narrower policies: tighter export controls on advanced chips and chipmaking equipment to authoritarian governments, a crackdown on "industrial-scale distillation" of frontier model outputs, and mandatory safety testing for sufficiently capable models whether open or closed. Resentment also stems from Anthropic's product expansion into partners' territory — the April launch of Claude Design knocked Figma and Adobe shares down, and Figma CEO Dylan Field said publicly that Anthropic had not been "consistently candid in their communications." The friction extends to Washington, where the Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic in February over restrictions on mass surveillance and autonomous weapons use, and top Pentagon official Emil Michael said there is "no AI company more hostile to the warfighter." Founders and researchers have increasingly cited cost and control as reasons to shift workloads toward cheaper open-weight models from rival labs.

Exclusive | Nvidia Bets on Ilya Sutskever’s New AI Lab to Expand Compute Reach
Nvidia and Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence Inc. announced a long-term strategic partnership on July 27, 2026, with Bloomberg pegging Nvidia's investment at roughly $5 billion (Nvidia's own release left the figure undisclosed). The deal gives SSI access to Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform, which is expected to raise the lab's compute capacity by an order of magnitude, and the two will collaborate on Nvidia's current and future compute platforms using SSI's research insights. Jensen Huang said he is "excited to see what new breakthroughs SSI will discover" on Vera Rubin, while Sutskever said SSI has "research that is worthy of scaling up." SSI, founded in 2024, raised $1 billion at a $5 billion valuation at inception and $2 billion in February 2025 at a $32 billion valuation, backed by Andreessen Horowitz, Alphabet, Lightspeed, GV and Sequoia. The lab has stayed in stealth for two years pursuing a "straight shot" to safe superintelligence with no commercial products, and had previously leaned on a Google Cloud TPU partnership struck in April 2025.

Exclusive | Nvidia in Talks With OpenAI to Guarantee $250 Billion Financing for Data Center
Reports on July 26, 2026 said Nvidia is negotiating to backstop roughly $250 billion in lease and debt financing so OpenAI can take a 10-gigawatt data center campus being developed in southern Ohio by SoftBank's energy subsidiary. The full project, including the chips inside, is expected to cost more than $500 billion, with a first phase of about 800 megawatts targeted for completion in 2028; the $250 billion guarantee covers the lease and debt but explicitly excludes the Nvidia chips. Separately, Nvidia was said to be discussing financing OpenAI's chip purchases worth up to $350 billion. For OpenAI it would be a first major step toward owning its compute rather than renting from Microsoft, Amazon and Oracle; for Nvidia it locks in years of guaranteed demand. Neither company confirmed the talks, and skeptics including investor Michael Burry publicly flagged the circularity of a chip vendor guaranteeing its customer's financing.

Warsh’s Posture on Interest Rates Sparks Market Inflation Fears
At his July 29, 2026 post-decision press conference, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh insisted the central bank would not tolerate above-target inflation, declaring "There is no soft inflation target… not on this committee's watch" and pledging "we will deliver price stability" against the 2% goal, yet the bond market read his refusal to hike as evidence the Fed is falling behind. Long-dated Treasuries sold off hard as he spoke: the 30-year yield jumped from about 5.1% to 5.21%, its highest since 2007, while the 10-year climbed from just above 4.61% to nearly 4.69%, close to a one-year high. The FOMC held the federal funds target at 3.50%–3.75% on a 9-3 vote, with Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all dissenting in favor of an immediate quarter-point increase — the most unified hawkish dissent since September 2016. Warsh characterized the internal debate by telling reporters, "I asked for a good family fight and I got one," while the FOMC statement blamed elevated inflation partly on supply shocks including energy tied to the Middle East conflict. His remarks also touched off confusion over which PCE inflation gauge the Fed is actually steering by, an ambiguity that analysts said compounded the market's unease.

U.S. Stocks Slide After Fed Pause, Escalation in Iran War
U.S. equities were hit from both sides on Wednesday, July 29, 2026: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1,153.18 points, or 2.19%, to 51,594.14 for its worst session since April 2025, the S&P 500 dropped 1.52% to 7,316.15, and the Nasdaq Composite lost 1.74% to 24,442.94, leaving it more than 10% below its record high. The Fed's decision to hold at 3.50%–3.75% while three regional presidents dissented for a hike pushed the 10-year Treasury yield up about 9 basis points to roughly 4.70%. Simultaneously, President Trump declared the Iran ceasefire "over" and said negotiating with Tehran was a "waste of time" after the Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Air Base; CENTCOM said U.S. retaliatory strikes began at 8 p.m. ET. Brent crude surged 7.9% to settle at $90.74 a barrel, lifting Exxon Mobil and Chevron while industrials fell 3.42% and technology dropped 2.36%, with Caterpillar down 6.91% and Deere off 4.52%. Semiconductors compounded the damage as South Korea's Kospi sank about 6% overnight.

Value Outperforms Growth This Year, Powered by…AI Stocks?
Value's 2026 lead over growth is the widest on record: the Russell 1000 Value index is up roughly 18.3% year to date while the Russell 1000 Growth index is down about 3.6%, a 21.9-percentage-point gap that is the largest year-to-date advantage since at least 1991 (as of mid-July, the split was +17.7% versus +0.9%). The counterintuitive twist is that much of value's gain came from AI-linked megacaps themselves, because FTSE Russell's June 2026 reconstitution reassigned style weights: Apple became 46% value/54% growth and Microsoft moved to a 50/50 split, making both meaningful members of the value index. As a result, technology's weight inside the Russell 1000 Value rose from about 3.7% to roughly 18.6%, second only to financials. That means investors who bought "value" as a hedge against the AI trade were in fact partly buying the AI trade — a distinction that mattered acutely during late July, when the same names swung violently. Strategists argue the rotation is durable rather than a "flash in the pan," but the index plumbing means style labels no longer map cleanly onto AI exposure.

The Stock Market Is Not Your Rich Uncle
The underlying argument — that households should not treat equities as a benefactor obliged to fund their spending and retirement — lands at a moment of record dependence on stocks. Equities reached 45.76% of U.S. household financial assets in the first quarter of 2026, near an all-time series high and up from 43.09% a year earlier, with households holding roughly $55.15 trillion in corporate equities and fund shares as total net worth hit a record $183 trillion. Those gains are extraordinarily concentrated: the top 1% of households own 50.2% of equities and fund shares and the richest 10% own about 87%, while the bottom half owns 1.1%. Meanwhile the buffer most families would need if the market stopped cooperating is thin — the personal savings rate has fallen to about 3.0% versus a 6.2% historical average, and the median 401(k) balance for workers aged 44–59 is roughly $64,300, about 40% of the ~$168,000 implied by a 6x-salary benchmark at age 50. Academic work on the "household equity share" finds the metric is a negative predictor of excess market returns over the following five years, meaning today's record allocation is itself a warning that the market is unlikely to play generous relative.

Investors Rotate Out of Chip Stocks
The world's largest semiconductor companies shed more than $1 trillion — by some counts about $1.3 trillion — of market value in just a handful of sessions through July 29, 2026, as investors questioned whether trillions in AI infrastructure capex will earn an adequate return. Nvidia alone lost roughly $238 billion from the prior Friday's close, while SK Hynix, Samsung Electronics and Micron dropped about $176 billion, $173 billion and $113 billion respectively; Nvidia, SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron, AMD and TSMC each shed over $100 billion. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 24% from its late-June peak and was down 19% in July, on pace for its worst month since 2008, with SK Hynix's plan to lift 2026 capex 50% to $31 billion cited as a trigger. Money moved into defensives and financials — U.S. equity funds took in about $1.96 billion for financials and $1.47 billion for healthcare in one early-July week, and the XLF and XLV sector ETFs pushed to record highs led by insurers. Morningstar chief equity strategist Michael Field called the move "driven largely by sentiment rather than fundamentals… Simply put, it's loss of confidence," while Forrester's Charlie Dai said investors feared AI spending was "peaking faster than expected."

Memory names took the deepest damage in the July 28–29, 2026 rout rather than the AI logic leaders. In Asia on July 28, SK Hynix closed down 14.65% and Samsung Electronics fell more than 13%, while Japan's Kioxia plunged over 18%, Tokyo Electron dropped 10.96% and Advantest slid more than 10%. The ‘Unsettled’ Vibe That’s Creeping Through the Markets
Wall Street closed the week of July 20-24, 2026 with its second straight weekly decline, the S&P 500 finishing Friday at 7,411.98 (+0.05%) while the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.64% to 24,975.82 and the Dow rose 235.60 points to 51,947.25. The unease is not about the headline level — the S&P 500 is still up more than 8% in 2026 and second-quarter earnings are tracking a 26.5% year-over-year gain per LSEG IBES — but about what sits underneath it: Alphabet dropped 7.1% on July 23, its worst day since May 7, 2025, and Tesla sank 15%, its biggest one-day fall since March 10, 2025, both punished for AI capital spending rather than missed profits. Breadth has diverged on 52 trading days so far this year, matching the record set in 2000, and the strain has spread into credit, where hyperscalers have issued $182 billion of bonds in 2026 to fund data centers and Oracle's CDS spread hit a seven-year high near 75 basis points. Man Group chief market strategist Kristina Hooper called the tape "very frothy" and said investors "are, to a certain extent, walking on eggshells," while the calendar adds pressure: August through October is historically the S&P 500's weakest three-month stretch since 1928, positive only 55.1% of the time.

The Everyday Guide to Supersizing Your Retirement Account
For 2026 the IRS raised the 401(k)/403(b)/457/TSP elective deferral limit to $24,500 and the IRA limit to $7,500 (IRA catch-up $1,100), with the standard age-50 catch-up at $8,000 for a $32,500 personal cap. SECURE 2.0's "super catch-up" for savers who turn 60, 61, 62 or 63 during the year is $11,250 instead of $8,000, lifting their personal deferral ceiling to $35,750 — though the feature is optional and only works if the employer's plan adopts it. A major change takes effect January 1, 2026: under Treasury/IRS final regulations issued September 16, 2025, anyone 50-plus who earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages in 2025 (box 3 of the W-2, up from the statute's original $145,000) must make all catch-up contributions on a Roth after-tax basis. The overall Section 415(c) annual-additions cap — employee deferrals plus employer match plus after-tax money — rises to $72,000 for 2026 ($80,000 with the age-50 catch-up, $83,250 with the 60-63 catch-up). That is what makes the mega-backdoor Roth work: a plan permitting after-tax contributions plus in-service withdrawals or in-plan Roth conversions can absorb up to $47,500 of after-tax dollars ($72,000 minus $24,500, reduced dollar-for-dollar by any employer match) and convert it to Roth.

Robinhood reworked its $695-annual-fee Platinum Card in July 2026 after cardholders complained the perks felt like a restrictive "coupon book" tied to spending minimums; the company began sending sign-up offers to high-spending Gold members around July 22, 2026, with more invitations to follow. Exclusive | Federal Probe Into Dodgers Owner’s Financial Empire Was Sparked by Whistleblower
Federal prosecutors in Manhattan, working in parallel with the SEC, are investigating potential financial improprieties at Mark Walter's Guggenheim Partners and two insurers he controls, after an internal whistleblower complaint questioned how Guggenheim Investments booked revenue tied to the insurers. Delaware Life Insurance Co. ($69 billion in assets as of March 2026) and Clear Spring Life and Annuity Co. ($16 billion), both under the Group 1001 umbrella, received grand jury subpoenas in February 2026, disclosed in regulatory filings dated June 26, 2026; the FBI executed at least one search warrant in September 2025 to seize a mobile phone. At the center is disclosure of related-party lending: Delaware Life had told regulators that about 3% of its investments, roughly $1.4 billion, involved parties connected to Walter's other ventures, then restated the figure to more than $17 billion, or at least 39% of invested assets — an additional roughly $16 billion of affiliate-linked private credit that had gone unreported. Prosecutors opened a separate inquiry last year into Guggenheim's $362 billion asset-management arm, where Walter is CEO. Walter, whose TWG Global controls stakes in the Dodgers, the Lakers and Chelsea FC, has not been charged with any wrongdoing, and TWG says it is aware of and cooperating with the investigation.

The Price to Finance the AI Data Center Boom Is Rising, Just Ask Meta
BlackRock priced roughly $12.3 billion of bonds in the week of July 20, 2026 to fund Meta's roughly 1-gigawatt El Paso, Texas data center (codenamed "Sopaipilla"), with BlackRock's infrastructure and private-credit arms holding 80% of the SPV and Meta 20%. The investment-grade-rated paper cleared at a 7.534% yield — a junk-bond-like level — after early price talk above 7%, with investors demanding roughly 0.4 percentage points more risk premium than Meta got on its record $27 billion "Hyperion" financing through the Beignet Investor LLC SPV (80% Blue Owl / 20% Meta) that priced last October at about 225 basis points over Treasuries. Those Hyperion bonds had already slipped to roughly 96 cents on the dollar in secondary trading, signaling investor fatigue with long-dated AI infrastructure debt that in some cases runs to 2049 against GPUs with a 3-5 year useful life. The El Paso deal nonetheless rallied in early trading Monday, July 27, as the fat yield ultimately pulled buyers in. The repricing matters because AI-related debt is piling up at roughly $100 billion a quarter, with estimates of up to $800 billion of private credit needed by 2028 and $3 trillion of data center investment over five years.

How the Big Premium Credit Cards Stack Up Against Each Other
The premium tier has re-priced sharply since mid-2025: the Amex Platinum jumped from $695 to $895 in its September 18, 2025 refresh (existing cardholders hit at renewals on or after January 2, 2026), the Chase Sapphire Reserve went from $550 to $795 for applicants approved on or after June 23, 2025, and Citi launched the Strata Elite at $595 on July 27, 2025 as the Prestige successor. Each now leans on a "coupon book" of segmented credits — Amex advertises over $3,500 in value across up to $600 prepaid hotels, $400 Resy, $300 Equinox, $300 Lululemon, $300 digital entertainment, $219 Clear+, $200 airline fees, $200 Uber Cash and $155 Walmart+; Chase counters with up to $500 The Edit hotels, $300 flexible travel, $300 StubHub, $300 OpenTable dining, $288 Apple subscriptions and $120 Lyft. Capital One's Venture X remains the outlier at $395, where a $300 Capital One Travel credit plus a 10,000-mile ($100) anniversary bonus roughly covers the fee with no spending games. Current welcome offers run to 175,000 Amex points after $12,000 in six months, 100,000 Chase points after $6,000 in three months (valued near $2,050 at TPG's 2.05-cent Ultimate Rewards rate), 75,000 Citi points after $6,000, and 75,000 Venture X miles after $4,000. The cheapest way into the "premium" conversation is the Robinhood Gold Card, which charges no annual fee but requires a $50-a-year Robinhood Gold membership and pays a flat 3% cash back on everything, with 5x on travel booked in Robinhood's portal.

Canada to Cease Compelling U.S. Streamers to Help Finance Domestic Broadcasting
Under the Online Streaming Act (Bill C-11, passed 2023), the CRTC ruled on June 4, 2024 (Broadcasting Regulatory Policy CRTC 2024-121) that foreign-owned streamers with at least $25 million in annual Canadian revenues must pay a 5% "base contribution" of those revenues, an estimated $200 million a year earmarked for local news, French-language, Indigenous and OLMC content starting in the 2024-25 broadcast year. The base payments were stayed in December 2024, and in May 2026 the CRTC went further with Broadcasting Regulatory Policy CRTC 2026-96, tripling the requirement to 15% of Canadian revenues — a package the regulator valued at over $2 billion, with services above $100 million in revenue required to steer 30% of spending to Canadian broadcaster and independent-producer partnerships. CRTC broadcasting vice-president Scott Shortliffe said of the rules, "We believe that they will be respected by these companies." That framework is now dead: a Department of Justice letter dated July 17, 2026 from Senior General Counsel Michael Morris, filed with the Federal Court of Appeal, states that "the Government's intention is to eliminate the base contribution requirement on streaming services and to provide government funding to replace those contributions," following the government's June 3, 2026 announcement and a June 29, 2026 direction from Justice David Stratas. Ottawa plans to publish the new policy direction in the Canada Gazette Part I for consultation and substitute public money — Michael Geist pegs the replacement envelope at roughly $600 million a year for the audio and audiovisual sector — after the U.S. trade representative repeatedly called the contribution rules an "irritant" in bilateral talks.

NYSE Owner to Buy MarketAxess in $6 Billion Deal
Intercontinental Exchange, the parent of the New York Stock Exchange, agreed on July 30, 2026 to acquire electronic bond-trading platform MarketAxess Holdings for $167 a share in cash, an equity value of about $6 billion and an enterprise value of roughly $5.7 billion, representing a 33% premium to MarketAxess's prior close. MarketAxess shares surged about 28-30%, while ICE shares were roughly flat to slightly higher. ICE Chair and CEO Jeff Sprecher said the combination will "build the fixed-income ecosystem that investors have always deserved — one that is transparent, efficient, fully connected, and accessible to all," pitching a single platform spanning pre-trade pricing and analytics through electronic execution and post-trade compliance; MarketAxess CEO Chris Concannon cast the fit as complementary, pairing MarketAxess's credit-trading network with ICE's retail protocols and data connectivity. ICE targets $100 million in annual run-rate expense synergies within three years of closing and plans to fund the purchase entirely with newly issued debt — bonds, a term loan and commercial paper — returning to a 3.0x gross leverage ratio within 18 to 24 months after close. Both boards have approved; closing is targeted for the first half of 2027, pending MarketAxess shareholder and regulatory approvals, and the deal squares ICE up directly against Tradeweb and Bloomberg in electronic credit trading.
