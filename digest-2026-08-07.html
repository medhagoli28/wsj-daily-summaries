The Latest Scary-Sounding AI Milestone: A Brand-New Virus
Researchers at Stanford and the Arc Institute reported in Science (DOI 10.1126/science.aec2657) in early August 2026 the first generative design of complete, viable viral genomes, using the Evo genome language models fine-tuned on roughly 15,000 Microviridae genomes to redesign phiX174, a compact single-stranded DNA bacteriophage of about 5 kilobases and 11 genes. From thousands of computationally generated candidates the team synthesized and tested roughly 300 diverse genomes, and 16 of them replicated in E. coli and lysed the bacteria, with some designs sharing as little as 63% average amino-acid identity with natural proteins. Several of the AI-designed phages showed faster lysis kinetics than wild-type phiX174 or outcompeted it in growth assays, and a cocktail of the generated phages overcame resistance in three phiX174-resistant E. coli strains that the natural phage alone could not defeat — a proof of concept for phage therapy against drug-resistant bacteria. The work first appeared as a bioRxiv preprint on Sept. 17, 2025, and the team excluded human-infecting viruses from the training data as a biosecurity precaution. An accompanying Science perspective by Thomas Inglesby and Moritz S. Hanke argued that biosecurity rules and oversight need to catch up quickly as genome-design models become more capable and more widely available, since the same methods could in principle be pointed at human pathogens.

A User’s Guide to the Universe of Rogue AI Bots
The rogue-bot problem is now measurable rather than anecdotal: Cloudflare's investigation, published Aug. 4, 2025, documented Perplexity running undeclared crawlers that spoofed a macOS Chrome user agent (Chrome/124.0.0.0 Safari/537.36) across tens of thousands of domains at 3 million to 6 million requests a day, on top of 20 million to 25 million daily requests from its declared crawler, while rotating IP addresses and source ASNs to evade blocks — prompting Cloudflare to de-list it as a verified bot and ship detection signatures. Cloudflare's own telemetry shows the economics behind the friction: roughly 80% of AI bot crawling is for training, with search and user-action purposes each under 5%, and crawl-to-refer ratios running about 50,000:1 for Anthropic, 887:1 for OpenAI and 118:1 for Perplexity, meaning publishers give up far more content than they get back in traffic. Cloudflare has since moved to blocking training and agent crawlers by default for new domains while leaving search crawlers allowed. Congress responded on July 23, 2026 when Reps. Laurel Lee (R-FL-15), Valerie Foushee (D-NC-04) and Gus Bilirakis (R-FL-09) introduced H.R. 9915, the bipartisan Stealth Bot Prohibition Act, which would require bots to disclose their identity and purpose, bar residential-proxy masking, and let the FTC seek penalties reported at up to roughly $53,000 per violation, with state attorneys general also able to sue. The bill was referred to House Energy and Commerce and drew support from the News/Media Alliance, whose president Danielle Coffey, along with News Corp and Condé Nast executives, framed disguised scraping as an existential drain on publishers.

Inside the Long, AI-Powered Quest to Perfect Pringle-Making
Kellanova, the Pringles maker now owned by Mars, spent roughly $4 million to $5 million on an AI project with Siemens at its Kutno, Poland plant to make every crisp come off the line identically despite constant variation in incoming potato batches. Notably, the gains were achieved by instrumenting and modeling the existing line rather than replacing hardware.

Trump Tries Again to Limit Birthright Citizenship
On Aug. 6, 2026 President Trump signed a new executive order titled "Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship," together with a companion order aimed at "birth tourism," just over a month after the Supreme Court struck down his 2025 birthright-citizenship order on June 30, 2026 by a 6-3 vote. Chief Justice John Roberts's majority opinion held that the 14th Amendment's citizenship clause has an expansive sweep, conferring citizenship on anyone born in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. Legal analysts expected litigation almost immediately, on the argument that the carve-outs reach beyond the narrow exceptions the Court described in June. The Dividend Mind Trick
The column's underlying subject is the "free dividend fallacy" — the behavioral illusion that a payout is bonus money layered on top of the share price, when in fact a stock that pays $1 a share drops by roughly $1 on the ex-date, so collecting a $1,000 dividend and selling $1,000 of stock leave an investor in nearly the same position once taxes and trading costs are counted. The August 2026 backdrop made the illusion unusually expensive: the S&P 500's dividend yield stood at 1.039% as of Aug. 13, 2026, the lowest in the recorded series and far below its historical high near 6.66%, because prices have compounded far faster than payouts and the index is now dominated by megacap technology names that distribute little or nothing. That leaves the income trade-off stark, with the 10-year Treasury yielding roughly 4.65% — about four times what a broad index fund pays — and forcing retirees who built plans around equity income to rethink the strategy. Corporate behavior has reinforced the shift, since US buybacks have exceeded dividends since the late 1990s and JPMorgan estimated a record roughly $1.2 trillion of repurchases, 50% to 100% above the annual dividend payout. The practical cost of compensating by chasing yield is well documented: those investors end up less diversified, concentrated in a handful of high-payout names they often overpaid for, and carrying larger tax bills.

The stock still fell sharply on the day — reported between about 14% and 19%, to around $242.90 — because management disclosed that its largest AI-native customer would reduce usage starting in the third quarter, a cut already baked into guidance even though that customer had just signed a nine-figure renewal at lower, de-risked usage levels. Third-quarter revenue guidance of $1.135-$1.145 billion implies growth decelerating to 28-29% from 36%, with fourth-quarter growth implied near 25%, even as Datadog raised full-year revenue guidance to roughly $4.46 billion and adjusted EPS to about $2.52. Management argued the business excluding that one customer is growing at the same rate and steadily accelerating, with the AI-native cohort surpassing 750 customers, including 31 spending more than $1 million annually and eight spending over $10 million. The selloff dwarfed moves in observability peers Dynatrace, down 3.5%, and Elastic, down 1.2%, suggesting the market was pricing customer-concentration risk rather than sector-wide weakness.

Expedia Raises Revenue View on Higher Profit, Revenue
Expedia Group posted second-quarter 2026 results on Aug. 5, 2026 for the period ended June 30, with revenue of $4.315 billion, up 14% year over year and ahead of the roughly $4.17 billion consensus, and adjusted EBITDA up 23% with 196 basis points of margin expansion. Total gross bookings rose 12%, but the mix mattered: the business-to-business segment, which powers travel booking for airlines, banks and other partners, grew gross bookings 21% while the consumer brands (Expedia, Hotels.com, Vrbo) grew 8%. On the strength of that quarter, Expedia raised full-year 2026 guidance to revenue growth of 9%-10% ($16.05 billion-$16.22 billion) and gross-bookings growth of 8%-9% ($129.5 billion-$130.8 billion), with adjusted EBITDA margin expansion of 1.5-1.75 percentage points — up from prior guidance of 6%-8% gross-bookings growth and as much as 1.25 points of margin expansion. For the third quarter the company guided to gross bookings of $32.2 billion-$32.8 billion, revenue of $4.65 billion-$4.75 billion and adjusted EBITDA of $1.51 billion-$1.56 billion. Management credited AI-driven product work and continued B2B marketplace expansion for the acceleration.

Bloomin’ Brands Says Outback Diners Are Buying Pricier Steaks
Bloomin' Brands reported second-quarter 2026 results (quarter ended June 28, 2026) with revenue of $1.0158 billion, up 1.3% year over year, and adjusted diluted EPS of $0.39 versus $0.32 a year earlier, beating the roughly $0.29 analysts expected by about 34%. U.S. comparable restaurant sales rose 2.3%, led by Bonefish Grill at 8.1%, with Outback Steakhouse up 1.4%, Carrabba's up 1.7% and Fleming's up 1.6% — growth driven by higher average check rather than traffic, which fell 1.9% company-wide. Management said guests were trading up into premium steak cuts more frequently than 2025 testing had projected, which let the company cut planned menu and mix investment to roughly $4 million from $18 million and trim full-year Outback turnaround spending to $36 million from $50 million, while pushing expected check growth up to about 3.5% from 2.5%. Restaurant-level operating margin expanded to 12.4% from 12.0%, and the company raised full-year 2026 adjusted diluted EPS guidance to $0.90-$1.00 from $0.75-$0.90 and GAAP EPS guidance to $0.85-$0.95. Shares surged more than 30% in premarket trading, to about $11.60 from $8.92, against a balance sheet holding $66.6 million of cash and $702.8 million of total debt across 1,084 U.S. restaurants.

Fox Will Keep NFL Rights Deal Through 2029
On Fox Corp.'s Aug. 6, 2026 earnings call, CEO Lachlan Murdoch said the company would not amend its NFL media rights agreement early, noting that its existing contractual relationship runs through the completion of the 2029 season and that Fox would engage with the league closer to the 2030 season, the customary timetable. Fox pays roughly $2.25 billion a year for the exclusive NFC Sunday afternoon package under the 11-year agreement announced in March 2021, which began with the 2023 season, runs through 2033, and carries four Super Bowls in 2023, 2025, 2029 and 2033 plus expanded digital rights including NFL content on Tubi. That contract contains a one-time NFL termination option after year seven covering the 2030-2033 seasons — the opt-out at the center of the story — and the league had held preliminary talks with both Fox and CBS parent Paramount Skydance about eliminating the opt-out in exchange for higher rights fees. Murdoch described the NFL relationship as incredibly positive and said he sees a clear path forward through the opt-out, effectively deferring any renegotiation for roughly three more years and blocking the league's attempt to reprice the package early. Fox separately added two national game windows for the coming season and struck a multiyear deal to broadcast NFL games in Mexico.

Molson Coors Sales Fall Amid Demand Challenges, Supply Costs
Molson Coors reported second-quarter 2026 net sales of $3.0965 billion, down 3.3% as reported and 3.6% in constant currency, as financial volume fell 5.4% and brand volume dropped 4.8%, more than offsetting a 1.8% benefit from pricing and sales mix. GAAP net income attributable to the company fell 46.0% to $231.7 million and diluted EPS dropped 42.3% to $1.23 from $2.13, while underlying pretax income declined 27.8% to $383.2 million and underlying diluted EPS fell 22.9% to $1.58. Cost of goods sold per hectoliter surged 12.1% on materials, logistics and manufacturing inflation, unfavorable premiumization mix and unrealized commodity hedge losses, including roughly $40 million of Midwest Premium aluminum costs in the quarter alone. Americas net sales fell 4.1% with underlying pretax income down 23.0%, while EMEA and APAC net sales slipped just 0.4% but underlying income sank 43.4%; net debt stood at $5.5815 billion, or 2.53 times underlying EBITDA, up from 2.41 times a year earlier. CEO Rahul Goyal pointed to global macroeconomic headwinds hitting both consumer behavior and input costs, and the company nonetheless reaffirmed 2026 guidance for roughly flat constant-currency net sales, plus or minus 1%, with underlying pretax income down 15-18% and underlying EPS down 11-15%.
