**2. Move 37 Is the Moment AI Changes Everything. It’s Suddenly Happening Everywhere.** — Move 37 refers to AlphaGo's stone placement in Game 2 against Lee Sedol in Seoul on March 10, 2016, a move human commentators initially read as a mistake but which sat in exactly the right spot roughly 100 moves later to win the game; DeepMind's own models put the odds of a human playing it at about one in ten thousand, and more than 200 million people watched the match. A decade on the phrase has become shorthand for AI producing results its creators did not anticipate, and 2026 supplied a run of them: DeepMind's AlphaEvolve found a way to multiply 4x4 complex-valued matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, breaking Volker Strassen's 1969 record of 49, and across 67 open mathematics problems it improved 23 and matched the best known result on 36. UCLA's Ernest Ryu settled Nesterov's 1983 convergence conjecture in October 2025 after 42 years using ChatGPT across roughly 12 hours over three days; in January 2026 a group including Jordan Ellenberg, Nicolás Libedinsky, David Plaza, José Simental and Geordie Williamson used AI assistance to uncover hypercube structure in Bruhat intervals of permutation groups; and in the February 2026 First Proof challenge models solved more than half of ten research-level problems. The same lineage produced AlphaFold2, which resolved the 50-year protein-folding problem, released structures for all 200 million known proteins, is used by more than 3 million researchers, and won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Skeptics including Akshay Venkatesh and AMS president Ravi Vakil note the models mostly clear a backlog of tractable open problems and raise unresolved questions about attribution, while Toronto's Daniel Litt has argued the shift may prove larger than the arrival of the computer itself. *Sources: [Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-ai-revolution-in-math-has-arrived-20260413/), [Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/10-years-of-alphago/).*

**10. The Latest Scary-Sounding AI Milestone: A Brand-New Virus** — Researchers at Stanford and the Arc Institute reported in Science (DOI 10.1126/science.aec2657) in early August 2026 the first generative design of complete, viable viral genomes, using the Evo genome language models fine-tuned on roughly 15,000 Microviridae genomes to redesign phiX174, a compact single-stranded DNA bacteriophage of about 5 kilobases and 11 genes. From thousands of computationally generated candidates the team synthesized and tested roughly 300 diverse genomes, and 16 of them replicated in E. coli and lysed the bacteria, with some designs sharing as little as 63% average amino-acid identity with natural proteins. Several of the AI-designed phages showed faster lysis kinetics than wild-type phiX174 or outcompeted it in growth assays, and a cocktail of the generated phages overcame resistance in three phiX174-resistant E. coli strains that the natural phage alone could not defeat — a proof of concept for phage therapy against drug-resistant bacteria. The work first appeared as a bioRxiv preprint on Sept. 17, 2025, and the team excluded human-infecting viruses from the training data as a biosecurity precaution. An accompanying Science perspective by Thomas Inglesby and Moritz S. Hanke argued that biosecurity rules and oversight need to catch up quickly as genome-design models become more capable and more widely available, since the same methods could in principle be pointed at human pathogens. *Sources: [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-sixteen-ai-viruses-route-drug.html), [Science Media Centre](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-generative-design-of-bacteriophages-with-genome-language-models/), [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.12.675911v1.full.pdf).*

**1. The Pros and Cons of Putting Extra Dollars in a Trump Account** — This is an advice column rather than a news event, weighing whether parents should add their own money to the new "Trump Accounts" beyond the free federal seed. Under the Working Families Tax Cuts, every U.S.-citizen child born from Jan. 1, 2025 through Dec. 31, 2028 with a work-authorized Social Security number gets a one-time $1,000 federal pilot contribution that does not count against the annual cap, and accounts began accepting outside money on July 4, 2026. Contributors can put in a combined $5,000 a year per child (indexed for inflation after 2027), of which up to $2,500 can come from an employer; individual contributions are made with after-tax dollars and get no deduction, while government, employer and charitable contributions can be pre-tax. Treasury and IRS guidance (Notice 2025-68) requires the money to sit in low-cost mutual funds or ETFs tracking the S&P 500 or a similar broad U.S. equity index, and bars any withdrawal before Jan. 1 of the year the child turns 18, after which the account is generally treated as a traditional IRA. That last point drives the main trade-off flagged by planners: earnings come out as ordinary income (with a possible early-withdrawal penalty), whereas a 529 pays qualified education costs tax-free and a custodial Roth IRA grows tax-free, so extra dollars often work harder elsewhere once the free $1,000 is claimed. *Sources: [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-issue-guidance-on-trump-accounts-established-under-the-working-families-tax-cuts-notice-announces-upcoming-regulations), [Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48910), [Charles Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/trump-accounts).*

Roughly 67% of boomer homeowners have lived in their current home 16 or more years, and 88% are in single-family houses, so a staged, multi-year downsizing is often the practical alternative to a single disruptive move. Reasons for staying are as much financial as sentimental: 55% simply like their home, 30% have it nearly or fully paid off, 31% say they couldn't afford a comparable home in their own neighborhood today, 16% cite home prices, and 8% won't give up a low mortgage rate. The economics cut both ways — the typical U.S. homeowner holds roughly $299,000 in equity (higher for long-tenured older owners), but smaller homes, condos and age-restricted properties frequently cost as much as or more than the house being sold once rising insurance premiums and HOA fees are counted. The demographic pressure is real, with roughly 12,000 Americans turning 65 every day, and about 28% of U.S. three-bedroom-plus homes owned by boomers in one- or two-adult households versus 16% held by millennials with children. *Sources: [Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/news/baby-boomer-homeowners-never-sell/), [The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/state-watch/5361274-baby-boomers-survey-housing-market-challenges/), [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/money/personal-finance/downsize-home-in-retirement/).*
