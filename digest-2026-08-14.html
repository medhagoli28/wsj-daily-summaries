Businesses Are Spending Big on Quantum
Corporate quantum budgets have moved from experimental line items to serious capital allocation: McKinsey's Quantum Technology Monitor 2026 found total quantum investment hit $12.6 billion in 2025, a 6.3-fold jump over the prior year, with private capital supplying 97% of funding versus 67% in 2024 and roughly 60% of the total concentrated in just ten deals. Among companies McKinsey analyzed, 33% now budget more than $10 million a year for quantum initiatives and 7% spend over $50 million, with the largest single corporate budget reaching $200 million. McKinsey revised its 2035 quantum computing market forecast sharply upward to $43-71 billion (from $28 billion a year earlier), projecting $1.3-2.7 trillion in broader economic value, while quantum firms booked over $1 billion in global revenue in 2025 and are projected to reach $4.4 billion by 2028. The spending is also getting more disciplined: a QuEra Computing survey of 291 respondents across 25+ countries, fielded in December 2025, found 44% expect budget increases in 2026 against 46% flat and 10% declining, with government mandates and grants cited by 28% as the top driver of increases. A separate IQM/Quantum Insider study of 107 senior practitioners underlined the gap between money and maturity — 89% report hands-on quantum activity but only 13% have any production deployment and just 9% run resourced IP programs.

Apple Showcases Texas Mac Mini Factory, New Manufacturing School
Apple opened a 20,000-square-foot Advanced Manufacturing Center in Houston on August 13, 2026, its second U.S. manufacturing training site after the Detroit Apple Manufacturing Academy, which has trained nearly 1,000 workers, students, and entrepreneurs since August 2025. The center sits inside the Houston plant where Apple already builds advanced AI servers — a facility Apple says it stood up, put into production, and shipped first units from in under nine months after investing "hundreds of millions of dollars"; Mac mini assembly begins later in 2026 in a newly built 170,000-square-foot section of the Foxconn site, the first time the Mac mini will be made in the U.S. The free curriculum covers printed circuit board assembly design, machine-learning-driven quality control, and advanced automation, with participants working on holographic lab tables and real factory-floor equipment. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Senator Ted Cruz, Houston Mayor John Whitmire, Representative Christian Menefee, and Harris County Commissioner Rodney Ellis attended alongside Tim Cook and the first cohort of small and medium-sized business participants. The center is positioned as part of Apple's $600 billion commitment to U.S. manufacturing partners.

OpenAI Chief Revenue Officer to Depart After Less Than a Year
OpenAI Chief Revenue Officer Denise Dresser is leaving after roughly nine months in the job, having joined in December 2025 following more than a decade as a Salesforce executive; the company said she is departing "to pursue other opportunities." Her replacement is Dali Rajic, formerly president and COO of cybersecurity firm Wiz, which Google acquired for $32 billion earlier in 2026. The exit lands in the middle of a broad leadership churn: COO Brad Lightcap departed days earlier, Fidji Simo stepped down as CEO of AGI deployment and the company's No. 2, and co-founder Greg Brockman has absorbed expanded management responsibilities. OpenAI reports over one billion weekly active users and two million business customers, but executives have conceded the company "hasn't hit all of its revenue goals," and the shake-up comes after OpenAI confidentially filed its IPO prospectus with the SEC in June 2026 — a recent $7 billion employee tender offer has been read as a signal the listing may slip.

DeepSeek Lifts AI Model Prices Fourfold
DeepSeek is raising API prices effective 16:00 UTC on August 16, 2026, ending the promotional rates it had pledged in May 2026 to make permanent and introducing a peak/off-peak billing structure where off-peak costs half of peak. DeepSeek V4 Pro jumps to $3.96 per million output tokens at peak from $0.87 — more than 4x — falling to $1.98 off-peak, while V4 Flash goes to $1.32 per million output tokens at peak from $0.28, or $0.66 off-peak; peak windows are 01:00-04:00 and 06:00-10:00 UTC, with all other hours off-peak. Across models, token types, and time of day, the new rates run anywhere from 50% to roughly 1,100% above current prices. DeepSeek justified the move as a need to "allocate resources more reasonably." Even after the hike the models remain cheap relative to frontier rivals — GPT-5.6 Sol at $30 and Kimi K3 at $15 per million tokens — though OpenAI's budget GPT-5.6 Luna at $1.20 now undercuts V4 Flash during peak hours.

Rick Perry's Fermi Finally Finds a Customer for Its Giant Texas AI Power Bet
Fermi Inc., co-founded by former Texas governor and Energy Secretary Rick Perry, signed a $6.5 billion, 15-year lease with AI cloud provider TensorWave — its first binding customer — covering a 222-megawatt data center at Project Matador in Carson County northeast of Amarillo, with two five-year renewal options and rights to expand into two more data centers that would push the partnership past 650 megawatts. Delivery is phased starting mid-2027, and the site could eventually house tens of thousands of AMD Instinct GPUs; Fermi ultimately plans up to 17 gigawatts from natural gas, nuclear, solar, batteries and grid ties, and has filed federal applications for four AP1000 reactors. The deal follows months of turmoil: a prior tenant's $150 million funding commitment collapsed in December 2025, the company had no operating revenue as of March 31, needs more than $3 billion in additional capital for initial construction, and raised $431.25 million in convertible notes in July. CEO Toby Neugebauer was removed in April and later sued over it, CFO Miles Everson resigned the same month, and on August 12 Fermi named board member Lee McIntire — ex-CEO of CH2M Hill and of Bill Gates' TerraPower — as permanent CEO, with the stock up about 23% across the TensorWave and CEO announcements. Fermi also agreed to buy up to 2.5 million gallons of water daily from Amarillo at double the industrial rate, claiming its cooling uses 80% less water than conventional designs — a projection local critics dispute.

Chinese Censorship Is Leaking Into Answers From American AI
A peer-reviewed study published in Nature found that Chinese state-controlled media has seeped into the training data of Western models and measurably shifts how they answer questions about China; researchers identified more than 3 million Chinese-language documents in the widely used CulturaX dataset. Tested on their ability to reproduce distinctive state-media phrasing, Claude Sonnet, Claude Opus, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o showed rates between 3% and nearly 10%. The language gap was starker: asked the same questions in Chinese rather than English, models were far more favorable toward Chinese leaders — 88.2% of the time for Claude Opus, 84% for GPT-4o, 72.6% for GPT-3.5, and 68.8% for Claude Sonnet. In a controlled retraining experiment, researchers fine-tuned Meta's Llama 2 13B on 6,400 Chinese state-scripted news examples and the model gave a more Beijing-friendly answer than baseline nearly 80% of the time; after 64,000 such examples it described China as a democracy rather than an autocracy. Separately, Meta's Oversight Board tested 10 commercial models and found an average 34% refusal rate for requests to produce political criticism of governments in speech-restricting countries versus 14% in freer ones — and researchers say relatively simple fixes, such as greater transparency about data provenance, would help, but AI companies have largely not acted.

Exclusive | DeepMind's Hassabis Pitched AI-Oversight Body Before Shake-Up
Demis Hassabis went public on July 14, 2026 with a call for a U.S.-led global AI standards body modeled on FINRA — a private, industry-funded self-regulator operating under government oversight — and said he wanted it stood up before year end. Under the plan, frontier labs would voluntarily share models with the body up to 30 days before release for independent safety testing of dangerous cyber, biological, and "deception" capabilities, with the body also defining capability benchmarks for what counts as frontier-scale; once evaluations proved effective, passing would become mandatory for U.S. market deployment, and participating firms would publish system cards, harden cybersecurity, and fund safety research. Hassabis spent months in private consultations before going public, meeting Trump administration officials, rival AI lab leaders, and European government representatives, and the proposal has drawn support from Satya Nadella, Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Jack Dorsey, and former White House AI czar David Sacks, though critics point to FINRA's own conflict-of-interest track record. Roughly three weeks later, on August 5-6, 2026, Alphabet announced Hassabis would give up the Google DeepMind CEO title to become chairman of the unit and Alphabet chief scientist, with CTO Koray Kavukcuoglu taking over daily operations as SVP reporting to Sundar Pichai; chief scientist Jeff Dean left the same week after 27 years to co-found Discovery Loop, and Alphabet shares fell about 5%. The narrower claim that Hassabis personally lobbied specific Treasury officials ahead of the reshuffle could not be corroborated in accessible non-WSJ reporting, so it is flagged here as unconfirmed.

The trigger was the July producer price index released Thursday, Aug. 13, 2026: PPI final demand was flat month-over-month and rose 4.7% year-over-year, down sharply from 5.5% in June, while core PPI cooled to 3.7% from 4.7%. That followed Wednesday's July CPI, which rose just 0.1% m/m and 3.4% y/y (from 3.5% in June), with core CPI up 0.2% m/m and 2.5% y/y. The S&P 500 hit an intraday record of 7,814.88 and closed at a record 7,798.99, up 50.49 points (+0.7%), helped by WTI crude sliding to roughly $81 a barrel and easing Treasury yields. Futures markets cut the odds of a September Federal Reserve rate hike to roughly 32%–35%, down from about 55% a week earlier, with Richmond Fed's Barkin favoring holding rates and Cleveland's Hammack still pressing for an increase; initial jobless claims held at a 199,000 four-week average.

The Jobless Boom Has Arrived
The July 2026 employment report, released Aug. 7, showed nonfarm payrolls fell 23,000 against a consensus of about +80,000, with local government education shedding roughly 50,000 jobs and retail trade down 19,000, while health care added 22,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1% from 4.2%, but only because the labor force shrank by 264,000; average hourly earnings growth slipped to 3.2% year-over-year, the weakest since May 2021. Revisions cut May to +63,000 (from +129,000) and June to +20,000 (from +57,000), leaving those two months a combined 103,000 lower. The backdrop is an economy still expanding — Oxford Economics pencils in about 2.8% GDP growth for 2026 — while total 2025 job creation was revised down to roughly 181,000 (about 15,000 a month) and 2026 gains are forecast to average under 40,000 a month, with Oxford's Michael Pearce putting the break-even payroll rate near zero as net immigration collapses toward 160,000 a year. KPMG's Diane Swonk frames it bluntly: "Growth and labor market outcomes have decoupled," as AI-era firms do more with fewer workers.

Healthcare Investing Is Now an AI Short in Disguise
This is an analysis column rather than a single news event; the underlying data is healthcare's collapse as a defensive allocation in an AI-driven tape. Health care has returned roughly 2% in 2026 versus about 9% for the S&P 500, and the sector's index weight has fallen to 8.3%, its lowest since 1994; earlier in the year the XLV healthcare ETF was down about 7% year-to-date while SPY was up about 5%, extending a five-year gap of 27% versus 71%. The mechanism is arithmetic: S&P 500 Q2 2026 earnings grew 24.7% year-over-year, but stripping out just Micron and Nvidia drops that to 16.8%, meaning roughly a third of the market's earnings acceleration came from two AI-infrastructure names that healthcare has no exposure to. Healthcare itself posted an 18.2% year-over-year earnings decline in Q2 2026 — though almost entirely from one-off charges at Gilead and Merck, with the sector flipping to +6.6% growth excluding those two, and 100% of healthcare reporters beating estimates by an average 11.9%. The sector now trades at roughly 18x forward earnings against the S&P 500's 20.3x, so owning healthcare has effectively become a relative bet against the AI capex trade rather than a valuation call.

Wealth Management Has a $3 Trillion Problem: Investors Are Keeping Too Much Cash
Money-market fund assets hit a record $7.93 trillion in the week ended Aug. 12, 2026, per ICI, split $6.54T government, $1.24T prime and $147.9B tax-exempt — with retail investors alone accounting for $3.10 trillion, roughly the "$3 trillion" of household cash advisors say is parked too long. Capgemini's World Wealth Report 2026 found U.S. high-net-worth cash allocations fell from 28% of portfolios in January 2025 to 23% in January 2026, while equities rose from 22% to 27%, as global HNWI wealth hit a record $98.3 trillion across 25.3 million individuals (up ~2 million). Globally, HNWI equity allocations rose 3 points to 25% and fixed income 2 points to 20%, while alternatives slipped 3 points to 12% — but cash remains near-record by historical standards after peaking at 26% in 2024. The opportunity cost is the core argument: stocks have beaten cash in 86% of rolling 10-year periods and 100% of 20-year periods since 1926, and BlackRock has warned that "the opportunity cost of remaining sidelined could be rising." Capgemini also documents why the cash is sticky: exclusive single-firm relationships among U.S. HNWIs collapsed from 55% to 14%, and only 12% call their wealth-management experience seamless.

America's Mortgage King Lost $600 Million and Needed a Rescue
The subject is Mat Ishbia's UWM Holdings, the largest U.S. mortgage lender, which lost $603.2 million on interest-rate derivatives in the second quarter of 2026, turning the period into a $451.9 million net loss on $888.0 million of revenue and $39.7 billion of origination volume (adjusted EBITDA of $185.9 million). On Aug. 5, 2026, UWM announced a $2.05 billion strategic capital partnership — roughly $1.5 billion of preferred equity plus warrants from Oaktree Capital Management, $150 million from the Ishbia family's new vehicle SFS Group Capital LLC, and a planned $400 million rights offering to Class A shareholders. The terms are punitive: a 10% annual cash preferred dividend (13% compounding quarterly if paid in kind), two Oaktree board seats, consent rights over senior management and bylaw changes, an exit premium starting at 10% and rising to 60% by year six, and a mandatory buyout after seven years worth at least $600 million — with full board control passing to Oaktree if payments are missed. UWM simultaneously suspended its common dividend to prioritize debt reduction. Ishbia has framed the derivatives position as a hedge tied to the roughly $1.3 billion all-stock Two Harbors Investment Corp. acquisition signed Dec. 17, 2025 and terminated by Two Harbors in March 2026 (UWM is suing for over $500 million), while conceding "I'm sure we could have gotten cheaper capital elsewhere."

If You Get in a Car Crash, the Risk Is Growing Your Insurance Won't Pay
An independent Weiss Ratings study commissioned by Citizen Action of New York and released in April 2026 found that nearly 1.3 million auto liability claims — 48.3% of those closed — ended in flat denials in 2025, a 44% increase in the denial rate since 2005, when roughly one-third of such claims closed unpaid. Weiss analyzed NAIC Annual Statutory Financial Statements (Schedule P, Part 3B) from 2005 through 2025 for insurers with 30%+ of premiums in auto and at least $1 million in New York private auto premiums; the single largest carrier in the segment closed 55.5% of claims without payment. The report also notes that since 2015 major New York insurers earned $42.3 billion in investment and other income against $27.9 billion in net underwriting gains, arguing the incentive structure rewards slow-paying. Denials are rising even as pricing eased: Insurify data show the average full-coverage premium fell 6% in 2025 to $2,144 and is forecast to rise about 1% to $2,158 in 2026 (up to 4% if tariffs lift repair costs), with Washington, D.C. highest at $4,017. Insurers counter that claims can close without payment for benign reasons — another carrier paid, the customer withdrew, the loss fell outside the policy, or damages were below the deductible.

Private-Credit Firms Clamp Down on Loan Sweeteners in Fear of 'Shadow Defaults'
Fitch Ratings' U.S. private credit default rate hit a record 6.0% on a trailing-twelve-month basis for the period ended April 2026 — the highest since the index began in 2024 — and stayed at a record ~6% through Q2 2026, up from 5.7% in November 2025. KBRA's DLD Direct Lending Index, covering roughly 3,000 issuers and $300 billion of BDC holdings, showed a 2.3% issuer default rate as of June 16, 2026, projected to reach about 3.5% (roughly 111 issuers) by year-end, with the loan-weighted rate climbing to 2.5% in 2026 from 1.4% in 2025 and an estimated $7.6 billion of defaulted loans. The "shadow" gap is the concern: Moody's estimates distressed restructurings — debt exchanges and duress-driven maturity extensions — made up roughly 65% of all 2025 private credit defaults, and Lincoln International pegs implied distress near 6% against a headline rate around 2%. "Bad PIK" — interest deferred mid-loan because of liquidity strain — reached 6.4% of total private debt volume in Q1 2026, nearly triple 2021 levels, which is why lenders are now restricting the amend-and-extend, PIK-toggle and covenant-holiday sweeteners that mask distress; Reed Smith notes flatly that "there is no precise way to quantify the shadow default rate." Stress is showing elsewhere too: private credit withdrawals hit about $13 billion in Q1 2026 and BlackRock gated redemptions in a flagship private credit vehicle for a second straight quarter.

The Lakers' Owner Needed Cash. Then Came a $12.5 Billion Offer to Buy the Team.
On Aug. 12–13, 2026, Mark Walter agreed to sell the Los Angeles Lakers to Josh Kushner and Bob Iger at a $12.5 billion valuation, a record for any American sports franchise and $2.5 billion above the $10 billion valuation at which he bought control just ~14 months earlier. Walter, a Lakers minority owner with 26% since 2021, struck the controlling-stake deal with the Buss family in June 2025 and won NBA Board of Governors approval on Oct. 30, 2025; the Buss family retained just over 15% and Jeanie Buss stayed on as governor, though reporting indicates her role is not necessarily carried into the new agreement. The cash motive is central: Bloomberg reported that Walter and Guggenheim Partners are raising cash to pay down debt amid a federal investigation into his TWG Global conglomerate over roughly $16 billion in undisclosed loans to insurer Delaware Life, an SEC probe running since at least September 2025 that has included seizure of an electronic device. Walter and his firms deny wrongdoing, and he retains ownership of the Los Angeles Dodgers. The transaction still requires approval from the NBA's other 29 owners, which league figures describe as largely a formality given Iger's closeness to commissioner Adam Silver.

The Unexpected Joy of Downsizing Slowly in Retirement
This is an advice feature rather than a news event, and the underlying data show most older Americans are not downsizing at all: an Ipsos survey of roughly 4,000 U.S. residents conducted for Redfin in May 2025 found 33.5% of baby-boomer homeowners say they will never sell, another 30% won't sell within the next decade, and 44.6% of the Silent Generation never plan to sell — versus 25% of Gen X and 21% of millennials/Gen Z. Roughly 67% of boomer homeowners have lived in their current home 16 or more years, and 88% are in single-family houses, so a staged, multi-year downsizing is often the practical alternative to a single disruptive move. Reasons for staying are as much financial as sentimental: 55% simply like their home, 30% have it nearly or fully paid off, 31% say they couldn't afford a comparable home in their own neighborhood today, 16% cite home prices, and 8% won't give up a low mortgage rate. The economics cut both ways — the typical U.S. homeowner holds roughly $299,000 in equity (higher for long-tenured older owners), but smaller homes, condos and age-restricted properties frequently cost as much as or more than the house being sold once rising insurance premiums and HOA fees are counted. The demographic pressure is real, with roughly 12,000 Americans turning 65 every day, and about 28% of U.S. three-bedroom-plus homes owned by boomers in one- or two-adult households versus 16% held by millennials with children.

Cardinal Health Logs Mixed Sales, Sees Growth in New Year
Cardinal Health reported fiscal Q4 2026 results on Aug. 11, 2026: revenue of $63.7 billion, up 6% year over year, with GAAP operating earnings up 70% to $729 million and GAAP diluted EPS up 70% to $1.70, while non-GAAP operating earnings rose 30% to $935 million and non-GAAP diluted EPS rose 40% to $2.91 — or $2.60 excluding an IEEPA tariff refund benefit. Segment results were uneven: Pharmaceutical and Specialty Solutions delivered $58.8 billion of revenue and $645 million of profit (up 21%), Global Medical Products and Distribution just $3.1 billion of revenue and $150 million of profit, and the Other segment $1.7 billion of revenue and $183 million of profit (up 14%). For full-year fiscal 2026, revenue was $254.2 billion (up 14%), GAAP operating earnings $2.6 billion (up 15%), non-GAAP operating earnings $3.6 billion (up 30%), GAAP diluted EPS $7.23 (up 12%) and non-GAAP diluted EPS $11.26 (up 37%, or $10.95 excluding tariff refunds); GMPD full-year profit jumped 91% to $258 million off a low base. Guidance for fiscal 2027 is for 13% to 15% non-GAAP EPS growth to $12.40–$12.60, above the company's long-term algorithm, with Pharmaceutical and Specialty Solutions revenue up 3–5% and profit up 8–11%, GMPD revenue up 2–4% and profit of $200–220 million, and Other revenue up 11–13% and profit up 15–18%. The company also guided to $3.5–4.0 billion of adjusted free cash flow, roughly $1 billion of share repurchases and about $700 million of capital expenditures.
