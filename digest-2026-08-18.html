A $21 Billion 'Kids in Chips' Startup Is Scooping Up Nvidia Talent
Etched, the AI inference-chip startup founded in 2022 by Harvard dropouts Gavin Uberti, Chris Zhu and Robert Wachen, has reached a roughly $21 billion valuation in reports dated August 18, 2026, roughly doubling the $10.3 billion mark it set on July 23, 2026 with a $300 million Series C led by Sequoia Capital and backed by Andreessen Horowitz, SK Hynix and Jane Street. That $10.3 billion price was itself double the $5 billion valuation Etched carried in December 2025, and the company had been negotiating two concurrent rounds — one near $10 billion led by Sequoia and a larger one around $20 billion led by Jane Street. Etched emerged from stealth on June 30, 2026 with about $800 million raised, working silicon for its transformer-specific Sohu ASIC built on TSMC's 4nm process, and more than $1 billion in signed customer contracts. Its roughly 400-person staff has been assembled largely by recruiting engineers away from Nvidia, Broadcom, Google's TPU team and SK Hynix, part of a broader talent war that also involves Cerebras, Tenstorrent and Groq. The company runs a 2-megawatt data center at its San Jose headquarters and is adding an 80,000-square-foot, 10MW facility in Milpitas.

Unitree IPO Could Mark New Era for China's Robotics Sector
Hangzhou-based Unitree Robotics priced its Shanghai STAR Market IPO at 150.80 yuan (about $22.40) per share on August 6, 2026, selling 40.45 million new shares — roughly 10% of enlarged capital — to raise about 6.1 billion yuan ($905 million) at a valuation near 61 billion yuan, or roughly $9 billion, under ticker 688836. Subscriptions opened August 10 with payment due August 12, and the retail tranche was oversubscribed more than 8,000 times, a record for the tech-focused STAR board, with the trading debut expected in the August 17-21 window. Chinese regulators cleared the listing in a record 104 days, and Unitree becomes the first humanoid-robot maker to list on China's domestic A-share market. Unitree's 2025 revenue reached 1.71 billion yuan (about $257 million), up 335% year over year, with adjusted net income of roughly 600 million yuan (about $90 million), up 674%. The listing lands as China's industrial robot exports hit 6.29 billion yuan in the first half of 2026, up 18.6% year over year across 141 countries, with intelligent biomimetic robot exports topping 10,000 units.

Uber, Zipline Partner With Goal of Hitting 1 Million Drone Deliveries a Day
Uber and Zipline announced a partnership on August 17, 2026 to add autonomous drone delivery to Uber Eats, targeting one million drone deliveries per day by the end of 2029 — roughly 230 times the current combined industry rate. First deliveries are slated for later in 2026 in Zipline's existing U.S. markets, including Pea Ridge, Arkansas and the Dallas-Fort Worth metro, before expanding to dozens more cities, with Uber estimating five-to-ten-minute fulfillment windows. Uber is making an undisclosed strategic investment in Zipline, which closed an $800 million Series H in March 2026 at a $7.6 billion valuation. Zipline's aircraft have logged more than 2.7 million deliveries, over 20 million individual items and upward of 135 million autonomous miles to date; separately, Walmart said on May 29, 2026 that it had passed one million cumulative drone deliveries across 66 stores in four states using Zipline and Wing. CEO Dara Khosrowshahi framed the deal as part of a "hybrid delivery network" combining human couriers, sidewalk robots and drones, citing quick commerce as a larger market than food delivery.

Why Big Tech's AI Spending Is $3 Trillion Higher Than It Seems
An analysis of securities-filing footnotes published in mid-August 2026 found that nine large technology companies — including Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD and SpaceX — carry roughly $3 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments tied mostly to AI, split between about $1.2 trillion in leases that have not yet commenced and roughly $1.9 trillion in purchase commitments for chips, data-center construction and energy. That figure is about five times the roughly $600 billion in conventional capital expenditures the group reported over the trailing year, and roughly triple what the same companies owe under booked leases and long-term borrowings combined. Alphabet disclosed $811 billion in purchase and contractual commitments as of June 30, 2026; Meta reported $347 billion in leases yet to commence, with total off-balance-sheet obligations near $420 billion — nearly three times its reported debt; Oracle's commitments stand near $273 billion, up more than 30-fold in four years. None of this is improper accounting: under current standards, unstarted leases and purchase commitments are not booked as liabilities until the underlying asset or service goes live. The disclosures have drawn attention from bearish investors including Michael Burry, who argue the obligations are compounding faster than reported capex and understate the AI buildout's true leverage.

The 'Country Hicks' Who Refused $26 Million from an AI Data Center
Delsia Bare, 54, and her 83-year-old mother Ida Huddleston signed a $26.48 million deal for farmland near Maysville, Kentucky (population about 8,700) — roughly ten times its open-market value — before learning the roughly 216-hectare parcel from their 1,200-acre, 200-year-old family farm would become part of a 2.2-gigawatt hyperscale data center. They reversed the sale, telling the buyer's representative not to come back. Public records reviewed by reporters pointed to Meta as the developer, though Meta has said no decision had been made about building in the area; a 2.2GW campus would draw about as much power as nearly two million average U.S. homes. In spring 2026 the pair joined the group We Are Mason County, and in June they filed suit after an earlier March complaint was dismissed. Their refusal, covered widely on August 17, 2026, has become a rallying point in a rural anti-data-center movement that blocked or delayed 75 projects worth about $130 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone.

The Summer That America Became a Nation of Luddites
Anti-AI sentiment hardened into a mainstream political force over summer 2026, with an Economist/YouGov poll released in May 2026 finding more than 70% of Americans believe AI is advancing too quickly — 68% of Republicans and 77% of Democrats — and an NBC poll showing 46% hold a negative view of AI versus 26% positive. Local resistance produced concrete results: researchers logged roughly 100 new local fights in the first three months of 2026, with at least 75 data-center projects worth about $130 billion blocked or delayed in Q1 alone, matching the total for all of 2025, and polling showing 71% opposition to data centers in respondents' own areas. Flock license-plate cameras drew parallel backlash in communities from Georgia to Pennsylvania. The word "Luddite" itself became contested political vocabulary, deployed as an insult against AI skeptics and reclaimed by critics, while an anonymous-organized "Summer of Ludd" series of social-media-free events ran in New York City starting July 2026. Analysts on both sides of the aisle now expect AI and data-center siting to be a live issue in the November 2026 midterms, which decide control of Congress and several governorships.

U.S. Urges Apple Not to Buy Chinese Memory Chips
Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said on August 15, 2026 that the Trump administration opposes Apple sourcing DRAM from Chinese suppliers CXMT and YMTC, and confirmed that Apple had received the message; he had toured Apple's Houston manufacturing site two days earlier on August 13. Apple petitioned the administration on June 27, 2026 for clearance to buy RAM from CXMT as a hedge against a severe global memory shortage and surging prices, and CXMT rejected Apple's request for a discount on August 6. CXMT sits on the Pentagon's 1260H Chinese Military Company blacklist over alleged People's Liberation Army ties and faces possible Entity List designation, which would bar trade outright. A bipartisan group of senators sent Apple a letter in late July 2026 demanding it abandon the plan, with Chuck Schumer asking for a response by August 21, 2026, and House Select Committee on China chair John Moolenaar calling the purchase a grave mistake. Micron lobbied the White House against the deal in July; a block would benefit Micron, SK Hynix and Samsung, while reports on August 10 noted HP and Acer already ship CXMT memory.

Volume was light at 14.74 billion shares versus the 20-session average of 16.95 billion, with investors holding back ahead of Home Depot's Tuesday and Walmart's Thursday results.

Stock-Picking Funds Are Performing as Poorly as Ever
Morningstar's US Active/Passive Barometer for the 12 months through June 2026 found that only just over 40% of active mutual funds and ETFs both survived and beat their average passive peer, meaning a randomly chosen active manager had roughly a 60% chance of losing to the index alternative. Over the trailing decade the record is far worse: only about one in four active strategies survived and outperformed, and in U.S. large-cap growth the 10-year success rate was just 5%. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA data tells the same story from the benchmark side, showing 79% of active large-cap U.S. equity funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025 — up from 65% in 2024 and the fourth-worst showing in the scorecard's 25-year history. A few pockets did improve in the year to June 2026, with active global real estate funds posting a 73.7% success rate (up from about 15% a year earlier) and diversified emerging-markets funds hitting 70%, a 35-point jump. Morningstar's most durable finding was that fees predict outcomes: in categories such as U.S. large blend and mid-cap value, the cheapest active funds succeeded at more than double the rate of the priciest.

Stocks retreated from record territory on Friday, Aug. 14, 2026, after the Commerce Department reported that July retail sales fell 0.6% to $763.6 billion, the steepest monthly drop since May 2025 and a sharp miss against economists' forecasts for a 0.1% increase. Motor vehicle and parts dealers led the decline with a 1.8% drop, while nonstore retailers — the online category — fell 2.2% from June, following a revised 0.2% June gain that had been flattered by World Cup and Prime Day spending. A separate University of Michigan reading compounded the gloom, with preliminary August consumer sentiment falling roughly 8% to 51 and ending a two-month run of improvement. The S&P 500 closed down about 0.2% at 7,785.76, the Dow lost about 0.2% to 53,732.41 and the Nasdaq Composite fell about 0.5% to 26,729.16. The data fed into the following Monday's caution as well, with investors waiting on Home Depot and Walmart earnings for a cleaner read on the consumer.

Why Big Tech's AI Spending Is $3 Trillion Higher Than It Seems
An analysis of filings from nine large technology companies found roughly $3 trillion of AI-related commitments sitting off their balance sheets, about five times the roughly $600 billion of capital expenditures they reported over the preceding 12 months. The bulk splits into approximately $1.2 trillion of signed leases for data-center space that has not yet commenced operating and about $1.9 trillion of purchase obligations covering chips, construction, energy and services. Alphabet disclosed $811 billion of purchase and contractual commitments as of June 30, 2026, up from about $332 billion just three months earlier, while Meta reported roughly $347 billion of leases that have not yet begun. Because uncommenced leases and purchase obligations are not recorded as liabilities under current accounting rules, headline debt-to-equity and leverage ratios materially understate the obligations; a narrower Nikkei tally in July 2026 put five hyperscalers' off-balance-sheet AI debt at $1.65 trillion against $1.35 trillion of reported on-balance-sheet debt. Analysts at S&P, Moody's and Morgan Stanley have flagged the risk that these leases roll onto balance sheets as facilities go live, leaving lenders and insurers exposed if AI demand disappoints.

Madison Air to Buy Airflow-Technologies Company for $5.4 Billon
Madison Air Solutions announced on Aug. 17, 2026 a definitive agreement to acquire ebm-papst, the Mulfingen, Germany-based airflow-technology maker founded in 1963, for an enterprise price of $5.4 billion, or $5.0 billion net of future tax savings. The price works out to 14.6 times ebm-papst's forecast 2026 adjusted EBITDA of $343 million on $2.8 billion of revenue — a 12% margin — falling to about 10 times including synergies. Madison Air is targeting $160 million of annual run-rate cost synergies by year three and says the deal expands its addressable market by roughly $30 billion while vertically integrating ebm-papst's electronically commutated fan and motor systems and a portfolio of more than 1,200 patents, with data-center cooling a key draw. The company plans to fund the purchase with cash, debt and equity, expecting pro forma net leverage below 4.0 times at closing and about 2.5 times within two years, and says the transaction should be accretive to adjusted EPS in the first full year post-close. Closing is expected around year-end 2026 subject to regulatory approvals; MAIR shares fell about 5.5% on the announcement.

Wealth Management Has a $3 Trillion Problem: Investors Are Keeping Too Much Cash
Retail money-market fund assets hit $3.10 trillion in the week ended August 12, 2026, part of a record $7.93 trillion industry total, according to Investment Company Institute data released August 13. Money funds pulled in roughly $935 billion of new money during 2025 and are on pace for another $500 billion in 2026, which would push the category to about $8.6 trillion by year-end. That cash is a revenue problem for banks, brokerages and advisers, who earn far less on parked money-market balances than on managed portfolios, and advisers' cash and short-term bond sleeve has climbed from 17% to 21% of fixed income between January and June. The math is also turning against savers: the Crane 100 index yielded about 3.58% in early January with the fed-funds target at 3.50%–3.75%, and Fed projections point to roughly 3.4% in 2026 and 3.1% by end-2027, with money-fund rates historically capturing about 95% of any cut.

Nvidia Downsizes Plans for $250 Billion Guarantee of OpenAI Data Center
Nvidia has cut its financial backstop for OpenAI's Ohio data-center campus from a previously discussed $250 billion to under $120 billion, with reports on August 17, 2026 putting the figure at roughly $105 billion in credit support. The guarantee now covers only the opening phase — about 4.25 gigawatts of computing capacity — with an option for an additional 3.75 gigawatts to be decided later, out of a full 10-gigawatt buildout that would be the largest data-center project announced to date. The campus is being developed by SB Energy, a SoftBank subsidiary, and OpenAI is still negotiating a binding lease for the entire 10 gigawatts; Nvidia has separately weighed investing about $3 billion in SB Energy. The restructuring followed investor pushback over Nvidia's risk exposure to outsized vendor-financing commitments, which knocked the stock down as much as 5% when the original $250 billion figure surfaced. Nvidia did not immediately comment and OpenAI declined to comment.

How CFO of Baby Wipes Maker Honest Revamped Its Finance Office
The Honest Company's board approved promoting Curtiss Bruce, 53, its CFO since June 2025, to Chief Financial & Operating Officer on May 20, 2026, effective the next day, consolidating finance, accounting and operations under one executive while he retains principal financial and principal accounting officer duties. Bruce, a packaged-goods veteran with prior stints at Hain Celestial, Keurig Dr Pepper, Kellogg, Kraft Heinz and Mars, inherited the "Transformation 2.0: Powering Honest Growth" plan the board approved in October 2025, which exits Honest.com fulfillment, the apparel category and Canadian retail and online stores — roughly 20% of revenue — at a one-time cost of $25 million to $35 million against $8 million to $15 million in expected annual savings. The reshaping hit the top line hard before it helped: Q1 2026 revenue fell 19.7% to $78.1 million. By the second quarter ended June 30, 2026, revenue of $83.3 million was down 10.9% reported but up 6.7% organically, gross margin expanded 800 basis points to 48.4%, and net income rose $6.8 million to $10.7 million. Honest ended the quarter with $105.9 million in cash and no debt, repurchased 5.6 million shares for $18.7 million year to date, and raised full-year guidance to $319 million–$325 million in revenue with 5%–7% organic growth.

Goldman Sachs Is Doubling Down on Investor Hunger for 'Boomer Candy'
Goldman Sachs Asset Management announced on December 1, 2025 a roughly $2 billion cash-and-stock deal for Innovator Capital Management, the buffer-ETF pioneer, and closed it on April 2, 2026, absorbing about $28–31 billion in assets and roughly 159–171 defined-outcome ETFs plus co-founders Bruce Bond and John Southard and more than 60 staff. The combined firm now runs over 215 ETF strategies and about $90 billion in ETF assets under supervision, putting Goldman among the top 10 global active ETF providers. Buffered or "defined outcome" funds — nicknamed "boomer candy" for their appeal to near-retirees — have grown from about $200 million in 2018 to more than $85 billion across 470-plus funds by early 2026, up from roughly $78 billion and 420 funds at the end of 2025. Goldman's own organic lineup includes the U.S. Large Cap Buffer 1, 2 and 3 ETFs (GBXA, GBXB, GBXC), which track the S&P 500 and absorb the first 10% of losses beyond an initial 5% decline over three-month outcome periods. In April 2026 Goldman extended the formula to crypto with a Bitcoin Premium Income ETF filing that sells calls against Bitcoin ETFs, which Bloomberg's Eric Balchunas also labeled boomer candy.

On 'Billionaire Bunker,' Buying a Mansion Is Easy. Getting Into the Club Isn't.
(topic explainer) No television series by this title covering Indian Creek Village could be verified from non-WSJ sources — the only similarly named show is an unrelated fictional Spanish Netflix thriller released September 19, 2025 and canceled after one season — so what follows is the underlying real-estate story. Indian Creek Village is a roughly 274-to-300-acre man-made island in Biscayne Bay with about 41 buildable home sites, its own incorporated municipality and police force with 24-hour patrols reached by a single guarded bridge, arranged around a private 18-hole golf course whose club membership is separate from simply owning a lot. Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan closed in early March 2026 on an under-construction two-acre estate at 7 Indian Creek Island Road for $170 million, the most expensive residential sale in Miami-Dade County history, eclipsing a $120 million Star Island record set in early 2025. The sellers, Dr. Aaron Rollins and Marine Rollins, had bought the site for $30 million in 2020; plans call for a 30,000-square-foot, nine-bedroom home with a dock, pool and a 1,500-gallon aquarium. Jeff Bezos has assembled adjacent parcels since 2023, including one reported at $105 million in June 2025, and entry prices on the island have run from roughly $64 million to an $80–$120 million trophy tier.

ArcelorMittal Says Two Killed in Missile Strike on Ukraine Facility
A Russian missile and drone attack on Kryvyi Rih overnight into August 17, 2026 struck ArcelorMittal Kryvyi Rih, killing two people and injuring 13 to 14 employees and contractors. The strike damaged the plant's main energy facilities and blast-furnace production units, forcing a partial suspension of output while specialists assessed the damage and restart timelines. ArcelorMittal condemned the attack, describing the site as a civilian target in the mining and metallurgical sector, and noted it was the second missile strike on the plant after a 2022 attack that destroyed a rolling-mill workshop and killed one employee. The strike was part of a broader wave: President Volodymyr Zelensky said Russia launched more than 1,550 attack drones, nearly 1,560 aerial bombs and 62 missiles, mostly ballistic, at 13 Ukrainian regions over the preceding week.

Trump Family's World Liberty Financial Gets Preliminary Approval to Launch a Bank
The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency granted preliminary conditional approval on August 14, 2026 for World Liberty Trust Company, National Association, a de novo national trust bank in Bay Harbor Islands, Florida, built to issue and redeem the USD1 stablecoin, hold its reserves and offer institutional digital-asset custody. World Liberty Financial states on its site that it is 38% owned by an entity affiliated with Donald J. Trump and certain family members. USD1, launched in March 2025 and currently custodied by BitGo Trust Company, had about $3.98 billion in circulating supply tracked on August 4, 2026 and roughly $4.013 billion of reserves against $4.012 billion of supply as of August 11. Preliminary conditional approval is only one step in a multi-stage chartering process — the bank must satisfy the conditions in the OCC's approval letter, documented in Corporate Decision #1385, before it can open. The OCC rejected objections raised over Trump family conflicts of interest, foreign investment and regulatory favoritism as grounds for denial, while World Liberty Trust president Zach Witkoff said the charter brings USD1 issuance, custody and reserve management together under OCC supervision.
