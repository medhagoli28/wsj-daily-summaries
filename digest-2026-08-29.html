An AI Oracle’s Rise and Fall
Oracle became Wall Street's AI bellwether after its September 10, 2025 earnings report, when it disclosed remaining performance obligations of $455 billion — driven largely by a $300 billion cloud-computing deal with OpenAI — sending shares up as much as 43% in a single day and briefly making Larry Ellison the world's richest person. Since that peak (stock above $345), Oracle has fallen nearly 60%, dropping to around $140-147 as of August 2026, as investors grew wary of the massive debt Oracle is taking on to fund AI data centers. The reversal accelerated after a December 2025 quarterly report showing capital expenditures jumped to $12 billion (from ~$4 billion a year earlier) while revenue missed expectations, feeding broader fears that Oracle's AI-fueled rally was among the first cracks in an AI investment bubble.

Microsoft Is Leaving Investors Flying Blind on Its AI Businesses
Microsoft's fiscal 2026 disclosures have drawn investor criticism for opacity around its AI businesses. In its fiscal year ended June 30, 2026, Microsoft reported Azure and other cloud services revenue grew 41%, but disclosed no dollar figure for Azure revenue, no prior-year comparison, and no separate expense or profit data for the segment. Regulatory filings also revealed Microsoft booked $24.1 billion in revenue tied to OpenAI, but didn't break out how much came from Azure compute consumption versus contractual revenue share — with some analyses suggesting OpenAI accounts for roughly 70% of Microsoft's actual AI sales. The lack of transparency has spurred at least one shareholder lawsuit alleging Microsoft failed to properly disclose AI-related risks and spending requirements.
MARKETS
U.S. Stocks Tick Down as Traders Weigh Inflation Data
U.S. stocks slipped on Friday, August 28, 2026, as investors weighed Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech and recent inflation data. The S&P 500 fell 0.25% to close at 7,711.76, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.52% to 26,402.42 (dragged down by chip stocks like Nvidia and Intel), and the Dow Jones Industrial Average edged down 0.02% to 53,559.99. Warsh said this summer's PCE and CPI readings, while better than expected, "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," and bets on a September rate hike jumped to 57% following his remarks. Despite the daily decline, the S&P 500 still posted a 0.5% weekly gain, its first winning week in three.
MARKETS
Boston Scientific Stock Price Drops After Cyberattack Triggers Global Outage
Boston Scientific disclosed on August 25, 2026 that a cyberattack had disrupted its IT systems, causing a network outage that hit order processing, shipments, and other global operations across its 127-country footprint. Shares fell roughly 3.4–5.8% in the following trading session (closing around $48.17 on August 26, down 3.38%, on volume about 48% above its three-month average), compounding a stock that had already lost nearly half its value in 2026 on weak Watchman implant demand and a lowered profit outlook. As of the reports, no group had claimed responsibility, the cause was still under investigation with outside cybersecurity firms, and analysts warned the disruption could last weeks and dent third-quarter revenue by hundreds of millions of dollars.
FINANCE
Gold Settles Lower on Warsh Concerns About Inflation
Gold prices tumbled roughly 3% on Friday, August 28, 2026, after new Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole speech since taking office in May to warn that inflation is not slowing significantly. Spot gold fell 3.13% to $4,457.17 an ounce, while Comex gold futures for December delivery settled 3.35% lower at $4,529.90 — a swing of roughly $173 from the overnight high near $4,688. Warsh said the Fed's 2% PCE inflation target is "a firm, fixed target" and that policymakers still have "work to do," noting PCE inflation last ran at 3.7% annually; his hawkish tone pushed the dollar higher and lifted September rate-hike odds from about 1-in-3 to more than 2-in-5, weighing on non-yielding bullion.
FINANCE
The Secret AI Lives of Gen Z
Based on multiple outlets covering this same trend as WSJ's "Secret AI Lives of Gen Z" piece: Gen Z is increasingly turning to AI chatbots for private, personal needs they don't disclose to friends or family. A Fast Company report (citing recent survey data) found that roughly 34% of Gen Z admit to confiding in AI chatbots about things they've never told another person, with 16% saying they discuss personal topics like mental health or relationships with AI frequently, and 33% doing so occasionally. CNN reported in March 2026 that Gen Z is "outsourcing hard conversations to AI," including drafting texts for breakups, apologies, and confrontations rather than handling them directly. Separately, Pew Research's February 2026 data found 64% of U.S. teens (ages 13-17) have used AI chatbots, with 28% using them daily; clinical psychologists quoted in coverage warn this pattern of quietly leaning on AI for social scripts can erode users' trust in their own instincts over time.
FINANCE
Uncle Sam’s $40 Trillion Tab Is Living Rent-Free in Your Mortgage Payment
The U.S. national debt topped $40 trillion in August 2026, and multiple outlets tie that milestone directly to elevated mortgage costs. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate has climbed to roughly 6.7%–6.77% as of mid-August 2026, per Freddie Mac data cited by NPR and Axios, driven by rising Treasury yields as the government issues more debt to fund a budget with deficits near $2 trillion a year. The Congressional Budget Office projects the federal government will pay about $1 trillion in net interest costs in 2026, with debt held by the public reaching roughly 101% of GDP. NPR reports that the roughly 0.7-percentage-point premium in current mortgage rates translates to an extra $511 a month for a typical homebuyer based on median home prices through June 2026.
