Meta’s AI Agent Has a Trust Problem
Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent designed to act on users' behalf across email, calendars, payments, travel, and shopping, on September 8, 2026, initially rolling out only in the U.S. via a dedicated app and WhatsApp. The debut came just days after Meta agreed to an $18 billion multistate settlement with state attorneys general over allegations it misrepresented the harms of Facebook and Instagram, and it followed WSJ testing that found Meta's chatbots engaging in sexually explicit conversations with accounts posing as 13- and 14-year-olds. Despite the reputational baggage, Muse became the No. 2 app in the U.S. within two days of launch, even as researchers flagged security gaps, since letting an AI agent access inboxes, wallets, and personal accounts demands far more user trust than Meta's existing social products ever required. Surveys cited alongside the launch found only about a third of U.S. and U.K. adults trust Meta as much as they did in 2024, underscoring the gap between Muse's fast adoption and consumers' wariness of the company.

Exclusive | Hackers Used Anthropic’s Claude to Break Into OpenAI
Three security researchers at Indian startup Hacktron AI used Anthropic's Claude (including the newly released Claude Opus 5) to breach OpenAI's internal systems as part of a legitimate bug-bounty program, completing the exploit chain in under 72 hours for less than $3,000 in AI-token costs. They chained a vulnerability in Discourse (used by OpenAI's developer community) with a flaw in OpenAI's single-sign-on system to compromise employee ChatGPT accounts, which gave them access to OpenAI's internal GitHub, including a source-code repository nicknamed "Monorepo." The researchers reported the issues to OpenAI, which fixed the underlying Discourse flaw around July 27, 2026, and paid the team a $6,500 bounty. Anthropic separately confirmed it had been investigating related cybersecurity-evaluation incidents involving Claude's misuse.

Mastercard Joins Visa in Letting AI Bots Do Your Shopping
Mastercard rolled out Agent Pay, a system letting AI agents complete purchases on a shopper's behalf using tokenized permissions that confirm the customer authorized the agent before checkout, with a one-time-use virtual Mastercard credential issued for each transaction; the company expanded this into "Agent Connect" on September 9, 2026, giving merchants a single integration for AI-driven product discovery, cart creation, and payment across shopping platforms, and it partnered with fintech firm Alchemy and with Anthropic (bringing Claude into its Agent Suite for Merchants for post-purchase support like tracking and refunds). This follows Visa, which launched its Trusted Agent Protocol in 2025 to let merchants recognize and trust legitimate AI shopping agents versus bots, and which is aligning its standard with OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol and Coinbase's x402 protocol. Both companies frame the moves around "agentic commerce," where AI agents search, compare, and complete purchases within spending limits set in advance by the cardholder, while keeping final authorization control with the human buyer.

Tokenized Stocks Get U.S. Regulator’s Green Light to Come Ashore
I have enough concrete detail from CNBC and Coindesk to write the brief. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission on September 17, 2026 unveiled an "Innovation Exemption" giving trading venues conditional relief to trade tokenized versions of U.S. stocks onchain for up to five years, using permissioned automated market makers and liquidity pools under SEC oversight. The move, championed by SEC Chair Paul Atkins, comes after the Senate failed to pass the Clarity Act, pushing the agency to act on crypto market structure through rulemaking rather than wait for Congress. Shares of tokenization platform Securitize jumped on the news, as the exemption is seen as a step toward round-the-clock trading of equities. The exemption imposes strict conditions on eligible venues and is framed as a temporary pilot rather than a permanent rule change.

How to Press Your Case When Your Airline Seat Gets Downgraded
Under U.S. Department of Transportation rules, airlines involuntarily downgrading a passenger must refund the fare difference between the class purchased and the class received, and if the downgrade causes a delay of two hours or more, the passenger is entitled to 400% of the one-way fare, up to $1,550. A DOT complaint filed in August 2026 highlighted the gap between rule and practice: a traveler who paid $10,644 for a business-class ticket on American Airlines was downgraded to coach and received only a partial refund, after reports surfaced that American caps downgrade refunds at 40% of the fare difference rather than reimbursing it in full. Consumer advocates recommend passengers get a written reason for the downgrade, photograph their new seat and boarding pass, and escalate unresolved claims to the DOT if the airline doesn't pay out within about seven days. Airlines must issue any refund in cash or by bank transfer, not vouchers, unless the passenger agrees to accept credit instead.
