Norway Aims to Open Debate on Use of AI Glasses in Public With Ban Proposal
Norway's government announced on October 5, 2026 that it will propose a temporary ban on AI-enabled smart glasses in selected public places while an expert group studies permanent rules for body-worn recording technology. The proposed ban would cover locations such as parks, beaches, museums, shopping centers, public events, schools, and playgrounds, targeting devices combining cameras, audio recording, and AI features, but it stops short of a nationwide ban and would allow exemptions for vulnerable groups and socially beneficial uses. Digitalization Minister Torgeir Micaelsen said the temporary measure is meant to "give us time to conduct a thorough investigation and have an enlightened debate that can lay a good basis for a lasting regulation," framing the move as a way to spark national discussion on devices like Meta's Ray-Ban smart glasses rather than an immediate permanent crackdown.

Exclusive | Oracle, Broadcom and SpaceX Seek Blockbuster Debt Deals to Pay for AI Chips
Broadcom has spent recent weeks arranging more than $50 billion in financing to produce custom AI chips for OpenAI under its internal "Nexus" program, aiming to deploy multiple gigawatts of capacity through 2029. Oracle is negotiating with Apollo and Goldman Sachs on an off-balance-sheet financing structure in which private investors would fund an entity to buy chips for a 1-gigawatt data center and lease them back to Oracle. SpaceX is separately pursuing roughly $40 billion in financing — about $10 billion in bank loans and $30 billion in investment-grade bonds, arranged with Apollo — to purchase Nvidia chips. The moves reflect a broader shift toward bespoke private-credit deals, led by firms like Apollo, Blackstone and Goldman Sachs, as traditional public debt markets strain to keep pace with AI infrastructure spending.

Samsung Expects Operating Profit to Exceed $80 Billion for First Time
Samsung Electronics forecast an operating profit of roughly 107.4 trillion won (about $80 billion) for the third quarter of 2026, the first time the company has topped 100 trillion won in quarterly operating profit and a jump of about 782% from the 12.17 trillion won ($8.8 billion) posted in the same period last year. The record guidance, released October 7, 2026, was driven by surging prices and demand for memory chips—particularly HBM and DRAM used in AI servers—pushing Samsung's profit margin to roughly 55%, the highest in its history. Samsung's semiconductor division is expected to be the primary driver, benefiting from the AI-fueled memory chip boom that has lifted prices industry-wide.

Exclusive | Fired OpenAI Researchers Ask Company to Preserve Visibility Into AI Reasoning
Three former OpenAI employees—Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni—sent a letter to OpenAI's board and safety committees this week urging the company and the broader AI industry not to abandon chain-of-thought monitoring as models grow more advanced, arguing labs still lack reliable ways to safely build or deploy powerful systems without visibility into how they reach conclusions. OpenAI said it had "parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information," alleging they shared such information with an outside AI safety organization, but added that it agrees with the substance of their recommendations. The letter also calls for greater industry collaboration with external safety auditors to avoid governance failures as AI systems become more autonomous.

Zuckerberg’s Biohub Partners With DOE, NIH to Invest $1.8 Billion in Biological Data for AI Models
The Chan Zuckerberg Biohub has partnered with the U.S. Department of Energy and the National Institutes of Health, alongside Meta, Google DeepMind, and Isomorphic Labs, to launch a $1.8 billion Virtual Biology Initiative aimed at building AI-ready biological datasets, announced October 7-8, 2026. Biohub is contributing $500 million, the DOE is committing more than $500 million over five years, NIH is contributing datasets and resources backed by over $500 million in prior federal investment, and Meta, Google DeepMind, and Isomorphic Labs together are providing $300 million. The project's goal is to create a predictive "virtual cell" model that simulates cellular behavior to compress drug-discovery timelines from decades to just a few years.

OpenAI’s ChatGPT for Teens Poses ‘Unacceptable Risk,’ Nonprofit Says
Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute released a report on Wednesday, October 7, 2026, rating OpenAI's ChatGPT for Teens an "Unacceptable Risk," arguing it should be restricted to users 18 and older until gaps are fixed. The nonprofit tested more than 4,000 prompts on accounts registered to 13- to 17-year-olds, both before and after OpenAI's August 18 rollout of the teen experience, and had the responses reviewed by child psychiatrists and a pediatrician. It found conversations involving suicidal ideation, self-harm, or disordered eating could run up to an hour without any parental notification, the chatbot missed more than one in four warranted crisis referrals, and the age-estimation system failed to reclassify adult accounts even when users said they were 13. OpenAI disputed the findings, saying the testing "does not accurately reflect how ChatGPT's teen safeguards work in practice."

Five Things to Know About Tokenized Stocks
Tokenized stocks are blockchain-based tokens, typically ERC-20 or SPL, that track the price of public shares like Apple or Tesla and are backed 1:1 by shares held with a regulated custodian, enabling 24/7 trading, fractional ownership, and near-instant settlement outside traditional exchanges like the DTCC. Kraken's xStocks, built by Backed Finance on Solana, launched May 22, 2025 with about 60 tickers but remains unavailable to US, UK, Canadian, and Australian users, while Robinhood switched on 200+ stock and ETF tokens for EU/EEA users on June 30, 2025 and has since expanded to over 2,000 tokens with a €1 minimum investment. On September 17, 2026, the SEC approved a temporary five-year "Innovation Exemption" letting permissioned Tokenized Securities Venues use automated market makers to trade tokenized NMS stocks in the US, provided token holders get the same dividend, voting, and liquidation rights as traditional shareholders. Public company issuers get 30 days to object if a third party tokenizes their stock without the company's initiation.

Trump Accounts’ Extraordinary Expansion Puts Individual Stocks Into Kids’ Holdings
The Treasury Department expanded the Trump Accounts program by automatically enrolling more than 60 million children, bringing the total to roughly 70 million kids with accounts, and issued temporary regulations effective September 30, 2026 allowing publicly traded companies to donate individual stock shares into the accounts, which must be held unsold for five years. SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell pledged one share each to over 2 million children aged 11-17 in lower-income households, while Michael and Susan Dell committed $6.25 billion to fund $250 contributions for 25 million children, with more than 10 million already receiving the money. Previously, the accounts were an optional, index-fund-based vehicle with about 7 million sign-ups and a $1,000 government seed deposit for newborns; the new rules let donated shares concentrate individual stocks like Nvidia, Caterpillar, Home Depot, and Tesla in children's portfolios rather than diversified funds.

European Stocks Should Withstand Bond Volatility
Rising global bond yields have pressured European equities through September and into October 2026, with the STOXX Europe 600 falling 2.4% in euro terms in September and touching a more-than-three-month low on October 2 before rebounding 0.8% that Friday as oil prices eased and softer US jobs data cooled Fed rate-hike expectations. The selloff has been sharpest in sovereign debt, with the UK's 30-year gilt yield briefly topping 6% for the first time since 1998 and France's 10-year yield jumping roughly 70 basis points in September to its highest since 2002 amid budget-deficit and political worries ahead of the 2027 election. European bank stocks bore the brunt, posting their worst weekly fall since April, with Societe Generale and Deutsche Bank shares each tumbling more than 5% in a subsequent selloff tied to fears over fiscal sustainability and political turmoil. Despite the turbulence, underlying eurozone economic data has remained comparatively resilient, which is the main reason cited for expectations that stocks can withstand continued bond-market volatility.

Exclusive | Wells Fargo Faces Regulator Probe Over Efforts to Boost Black Homeownership
The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development opened a fair-lending investigation into Wells Fargo on October 7, 2026, examining the bank's programs aimed at boosting Black homeownership, including a $60 billion lending commitment announced in 2017 to help at least 250,000 Black homeowners by 2027. HUD Secretary Scott Turner and Assistant Secretary Craig Trainor, in a letter to CEO Charlie Scharf, accused the bank of "sorting" homeowners by race and offering different mortgage products or terms based on race, potentially violating the Fair Housing Act; the probe also covers 2022 commitments of $210 million for racial equity in homeownership and $150 million to lower mortgage and refinancing costs. Wells Fargo had reportedly reached about 40% of its original $60 billion commitment and refinanced roughly 5,100 customers under a special-purpose credit program before commissioning a racial-equity assessment in late 2023. HUD said it is also reviewing similar initiatives at other U.S. banks.

Exclusive | Warren and Hawley to Probe Insurers Over Zero-Payout Claims
Sens. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) and Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) sent letters on October 2, 2026 to six major insurers—State Farm, Allstate, USAA, Farmers, Liberty Mutual, and American Family—demanding data on claims closed without payment. The senators cited figures showing 44% of homeowners claims resolved in 2025 ended in no payout, up from 36% a decade earlier, while 45% of auto liability and medical claims went unpaid in 2025, up from about 35% in 2016. They asked the insurers to disclose denial rates and whether adjuster or employee compensation is tied to claim decisions or payout amounts. Warren and Hawley said the trend raises "serious questions about whether consumers can trust their insurance companies" amid what they called increasingly aggressive claims-handling tactics.
