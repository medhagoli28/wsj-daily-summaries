U.S. Defense Startups Flock to Taiwan to Build China-Free Drones
U.S. defense startups including Anduril and Shield AI have signed at least 13 deals with Taiwanese manufacturers since September 2025 to build drone supply chains free of Chinese components, with Anduril's Taiwan drone contracts surpassing $1 billion and Shield AI now sourcing 16% of its V-BAT parts from the island. Taiwan is positioning itself as a rare "China-free" drone manufacturing hub, offering components at roughly 25% above Chinese prices while leveraging its semiconductor and electronics base; the government has designated drones as one of five "trusted industries" and plans to invest about NT$44.2 billion (roughly $1.4 billion) in the sector between 2025 and 2030. Taiwanese firm Thunder Tiger became the first Asian company cleared to supply drones for U.S. military use and won an initial order for 1,520 units under the Pentagon's Drone Dominance Program, while also announcing a joint venture to build drone motors in Ohio. The push is also backed by the U.S. 2026 NDAA, which for the first time includes a provision supporting U.S.-Taiwan co-production of uncrewed systems.

AI Chips’ New Secret Sauce Is Packaging
Advanced packaging — not raw transistor fabrication — has become the binding constraint on AI chip production, as TSMC's CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate) technology, which fuses logic dies with high-bandwidth memory on a silicon interposer, struggles to keep pace with demand; CoWoS capacity is growing at roughly an 80% compound annual growth rate yet remains fully booked through at least 2026 and reportedly into 2027. TSMC controls an estimated 90% of advanced packaging capacity for AI chips, and Nvidia alone has locked up more than 50% of that allocation, squeezing out rivals like AMD and Intel. CNBC reported on April 8, 2026 that Nvidia has been snapping up packaging capacity as TSMC expands production in the U.S. to relieve the constraint.

Video Cameras Are Everywhere—and Now They Are Being Hacked for War
Russian intelligence operatives have been systematically hijacking internet-connected security cameras, doorbells, and baby monitors across Europe and Ukraine—exploiting default passwords and outdated firmware among roughly 1.2 billion internet-connected cameras worldwide—to monitor military transport routes and arms shipments bound for Kyiv, according to the Wall Street Journal. Ukraine's Security Service (SBU) says it has identified and secured, reoriented, or blocked about 40,000 cameras since Russia's 2022 invasion that could otherwise have let Russian forces spot targets and correct missile strikes, including webcams in Kyiv residential areas accessed in 2024. The tactic runs both ways: a pro-Ukrainian group called Team OneFist reportedly hijacked a camera feed in Crimea in late 2022 to track a Russian helicopter and uncover a previously unknown military base. Image-recognition software is increasingly used to automate searches through hijacked feeds for military vehicles and cargo.

The U.S. Army’s Desert Tech Test Shows Long Road to AI Warfare
The U.S. Army's Project Convergence Capstone 6 exercise ran July 20–29, 2026 at the National Training Center in California's Mojave Desert, involving roughly 9,700 soldiers (including allies from Australia, Britain, Canada and New Zealand) testing more than 90 technologies across over 1,000 square miles in temperatures around 110°F. Drones from companies including Anduril and Theiss UAV Solutions suffered overheating, takeoffs delayed up to ten minutes, and battery conservation that cut flight times roughly in half, while many systems still lacked full autonomy and required manual control with soldiers carrying spare batteries and parts. The exercise also exposed communication dropouts, vulnerability to electronic jamming, and a steep learning curve for soldiers trusting and troubleshooting AI tools under stress, leading officials to describe the Army's AI-fusion command system as "ready but not done." Overall, the test underscored that despite high-precision AI targeting results, widespread combat-ready AI integration remains years away.

The Compute Gold Rush
The five largest U.S. hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle — have collectively lined up roughly $700 billion in capital spending for 2026, nearly double the approximately $410 billion they spent in 2025, with most of the increase going toward AI data centers, GPU clusters and the power infrastructure to run them. Nvidia remains the chief beneficiary of this spending wave as the dominant supplier of the GPUs underpinning these AI infrastructure investments.

The Sleepy Insurance Product That Came to Power Private Credit’s Ascent
Fixed and indexed annuities issued by insurers now owned by private-equity giants have become a primary funding engine for the private credit boom, with life insurers' private credit holdings reaching roughly $849 billion, or 14% of their general-account assets, in 2024, up from just 10% a decade earlier, and private illiquid bond holdings climbing further to about $807 billion by year-end 2025, a $122 billion jump in a single year. Firms like Apollo (via Athene, $331 billion in assets) and KKR (via Global Atlantic, $150 billion) use annuity premiums as long-dated, stable capital to fund direct lending, asset-backed securities and other private-credit strategies that carry roughly 80 basis points higher yields than public bonds, letting them offer more competitive crediting rates to annuity buyers while expanding their credit platforms. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago found this dynamic accounts for about 61% of the annuity market-share gains made by private-equity-owned insurers over the past decade, a pattern regulators have flagged as a growing source of interconnected risk between the insurance and private-credit sectors.

EU Aims to Put Brakes on Chinese Hybrid-Vehicle Market Share With Import Cap
The European Union will cap imports of Chinese-made hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles at 350,000 units a year starting as early as December 1, 2026, down from the roughly 800,000 such vehicles expected to arrive in 2026 (up from about 300,000 in 2025); the limit rises 6% annually and the measure runs for four years. Brussels is invoking a WTO safeguard mechanism, which doesn't require proof of unfair subsidies, after Chinese brands captured 25% of the EU's plug-in hybrid market and 14% of the conventional hybrid market in the first half of 2026, up from just 2% in both segments in 2024. The EU already imposes countervailing duties of up to 35.3% on Chinese battery-electric vehicles (on top of a 10% tariff) but had not applied similar tariffs to hybrids until now. Vehicles from Chinese brands assembled inside the EU are exempt from the cap, effectively pushing automakers like BYD toward local production rather than simply blocking imports.

Planning for College Aid? The Federal Student-Loan Rules Just Changed
Starting July 1, 2026, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act overhauls federal student lending: Parent PLUS loans are capped at $20,000 per student per year with a $65,000 lifetime limit, down from the current "borrow up to cost of attendance" model, while the Grad PLUS program is eliminated for new borrowers entirely. Graduate borrowers taking out direct loans instead face new caps of $20,500 annually/$100,000 lifetime for most master's programs and $50,000 annually/$200,000 lifetime for professional degrees (e.g., law, medicine), and a new $257,500 lifetime cap applies across all federal student loans combined. The law also narrows repayment options to just two plans going forward — a revised Standard Repayment Plan and a new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) — and bars new Parent PLUS disbursements after July 1, 2026 from enrolling in income-driven repayment, limiting them to the standard 10-year plan. Borrowers already in a program before that date can generally keep borrowing under the old limits for up to three more academic years or until they finish, whichever comes first.

Affluent and Older Americans Got Much Richer in Postpandemic Years
The Federal Reserve's triennial Survey of Consumer Finances, released in October 2026, found overall median family net worth rose just 2% from 2022 to 2025 to about $216,000 after inflation, but gains were sharply uneven: households headed by someone 75 or older saw median net worth jump 37% to $505,000 (fueled mainly by retirement-account gains), making them the wealthiest age group, while families headed by someone under 35 saw net worth fall 23%. The top 10% of earners saw median wealth climb 31% to roughly $3.6 million, and the top 0.1% more than doubled their total wealth since 2020, driven largely by stock-market gains for those already holding equities. Median family income did rise 7% to $82,200, with lower-income households seeing solid wage gains in 2021-2022, but those gains didn't translate into lasting wealth growth since they had little stock or real-estate exposure.

How 7.4% Mortgages Are Giving Buyers Leverage in a Stuck Housing Market
The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate climbed to 7.40% as of October 8, 2026, according to Freddie Mac — up from 7.28% the prior week and 6.30% a year earlier, marking the seventh consecutive weekly increase and the highest level since November 2023. CFTC Proposes to Expand Definition of Swaps to Include Prediction Markets Trading
The CFTC on Friday, October 9, 2026, issued two measures asserting federal jurisdiction over prediction markets: a proposed rule that would formally expand the legal definition of "swap" to cover event contracts tied to sports, politics, cultural events, and weather, and a concurrent interim final rule exempting traditional casino-style gambling, including sportsbook wagers and casino games, from that same swap definition. The agency argues these event contracts are financial instruments "commonly known to the trade as swaps," a move meant to resolve regulatory ambiguity and cement CFTC authority over platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket. The proposed rule is open for public comment, and the action comes amid an ongoing jurisdictional fight with state regulators, who contend that sports-related event contracts are gambling products subject to state, not federal, oversight.
