- **ETFs Often Cost More Than They Should** — Index-tracking ETFs that go even slightly beyond the broadest benchmarks often carry fees well above the rock-bottom levels investors expect, with inertia among the biggest drivers of excess cost: Vanguard's S&P 500 ETF (VOO), the largest ETF in the category with roughly $1.68 trillion in assets, charges a 0.03% expense ratio, while State Street's SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 ETF (SPYM) tracks the identical 500 stocks for 0.02% — a one-basis-point gap that, per a 24/7 Wall St analysis dated September 21, 2026, siphons about $10 more per $100,000 invested annually and roughly $1,200 in lost ending value over 20 years, despite near-identical returns (VOO returned 16.61% over the trailing year through September 18, 2026, versus 16.65% for SPYM). Fidelity's FXAIX charges even less, at 0.015%, underscoring how even "cheap" index products can be undercut by competitors tracking the same benchmark. CNBC has separately reported that while fee competition has driven headline expense ratios down industry-wide, analysts caution that price shouldn't always be the sole factor driving ETF selection decisions. _Sources: <https://247wallst.com/investing/etf/2026/09/21/voos-0-03-fee-hides-the-real-cost-state-street-now-sells-the-same-500-stocks-for-a-third-less/>, <https://www.cnbc.com/2026/01/02/etf-fees.html>_

- **How to Get All the Tax Benefits From Your Health-Savings Account** — The IRS has raised 2026 health savings account contribution limits to $4,400 for self-only high-deductible health plan coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, with an additional $1,000 catch-up contribution allowed for account holders aged 55 and older. To qualify, savers need an HDHP with a minimum deductible of $1,700 for self-only or $3,400 for family coverage in 2026, and out-of-pocket maximums capped at $8,500 (individual) or $17,000 (family). HSAs carry a "triple tax advantage": contributions are deductible (even without itemizing), growth inside the account is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are untaxed, with unused funds and the account itself carrying over indefinitely even after a job change. _Sources: <https://www.cnbc.com/2025/05/02/hsa-limits-2026.html>, <https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/smart-money/are-hsa-contributions-tax-deductible>_

- **This Investing Strategy Is So Good You Can’t Handle It** — I have enough concrete, non-WSJ-sourced facts to write the brief. A new study by financial economist Andrew Ang, "Uncle Sam's Cut: A Century of the Federal Tax Drag on US Equity Returns," found that federal income taxes cut long-horizon U.S. equity wealth by more than a third, with the average pretax annual return of 10.47% falling to 7.00% after federal taxes — a drag of 3.47 percentage points — based on a simulated taxable investor holding the capitalization-weighted market portfolio from 1926 through 2025. The study, posted to SSRN in mid-2026, found dividend taxation was the dominant historical channel for the drag, that the tax hit averaged 347 basis points annually across rolling 30-year windows (peaking at 538 bps in 1936-1965 and easing to 165 bps in 1996-2025), and that step-up in basis at death can lower the drag for older investors (to about 103 bps by age 70). The research underscores that a buy-and-hold indexing strategy, while cheap on fees, still loses a large real-world chunk of its theoretical return to federal taxes once dividends and eventual capital-gains realization are factored in. _Sources: <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6847621>, <https://www.cxoadvisory.com/individual-investing/federal-tax-drag-for-passive-u-s-equity-investors/>, <https://www.forbes.com/sites/bobcarlson/2026/08/23/taxes-are-a-significant-overlooked-drag-on-investors-stock-returns/>_
