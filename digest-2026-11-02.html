The Shopping Bots Are Coming for Amazon
Amazon is racing to control the rise of autonomous AI shopping agents: it sued Perplexity in November 2026 over the Comet browser agent's ability to make purchases on its marketplace, citing computer-fraud claims and a degraded shopping experience, while also blocking Meta's new "Muse" shopping agent from its site. At the same time, Amazon has leaned into building its own agentic tools — it renamed its Rufus chatbot to "Alexa for Shopping" on May 13, 2026 (used by more than 400 million customers in the twelve months through October 29, 2026, with users spending 50% more per order), and in May 2026 AWS launched an "Agentic Shopping Assistant" that lets other retailers deploy a similar AI agent in about 60 days. The tension reflects a broader industry fight: Walmart has taken the opposite approach, opening an "Agent Gateway" that lets outside AI agents (via Anthropic's MCP) complete purchases with spending caps of $50-$100, while OpenAI's ChatGPT Instant Checkout and Perplexity's "Buy for Me" push to let bots transact directly, putting pressure on the roughly $74.4 billion U.S. retail-media ad business that Amazon and Walmart dominate.

Apple’s Growth Machine Is Slowing. High Prices Might Not Save It.
Apple's fiscal fourth-quarter revenue rose to $478.8 billion for fiscal 2026, up roughly 15% for the year, with iPhone revenue climbing 17% to $57.58 billion in the September quarter, but analysts and reports now flag decelerating growth, with projected iPhone revenue growth of about 14% next quarter down from 22-23% in prior quarters. Apple has responded by raising prices, lifting iPhone prices in September 2026 and separately increasing MacBook and iPad prices as CEO Tim Cook said higher memory and storage costs made increases "unavoidable." Despite this, Apple's stock hit a record high in November 2026, pushing its market cap toward $5 trillion after briefly touching that mark in July 2026, even as analysts at firms like Loop Capital and UBS warn of margin pressure and shipment concerns tied to supply constraints and rising component costs.

People Are Using AI for Divorce Advice and It’s Driving Their Lawyers Crazy
Family lawyers say a growing share of clients are showing up to consultations with legal strategies, draft pleadings, or arguments generated by AI chatbots like ChatGPT, often convinced the advice is sound before a professional ever reviews their case; one survey of 133 family-law attorneys found 80% had received client correspondence or filings they believed were AI-generated. Attorneys describe the chatbot-sourced guidance as frequently inaccurate — for example, one business owner was told by a chatbot he had an "easy case" for lowering spousal support without the bot ever reviewing his financial statements, and nearly two years and two law firms later he was still untangling the fallout. Separately, in February 2026 a federal judge (Jed Rakoff, SDNY) ruled that a litigant's chats with a consumer AI chatbot were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor work-product doctrine, prompting several family-law firms to warn clients that ChatGPT conversations can be discoverable in divorce litigation. Lawyers say the trend is adding cost and friction to cases, as clients often expect their attorney to simply rubber-stamp conclusions they've already reached with a chatbot.

Anthropic Investors Push for Cautious Valuation Closer to $1.5 Trillion
Large investors planning to buy shares in Anthropic's upcoming IPO have pushed the company to target a valuation closer to $1.5 trillion rather than the roughly $2 trillion figure floated earlier, following a sharp sell-off in AI and tech stocks in recent weeks; some investors have said the final price could even land closer to $1 trillion. Anthropic's last private valuation was $965 billion, set in a Series H round in May 2026, and reports in mid-August had suggested backers were eyeing $2 trillion or higher for the IPO before sentiment shifted. As of early November 2026, bankers and investors were still negotiating the target range, with $1.5-1.6 trillion emerging as the more cautious consensus figure some large investors are pushing for.

America’s Multi-Trillion-Dollar AI Buildout Is on the Ballot—and Wall Street Is Watching
In the November 3, 2026 U.S. midterm elections, AI data-center spending became a flashpoint, with voters in roughly 10 states deciding on dozens of ballot measures—CNBC and other outlets reported at least 44 such measures nationwide, nearly all of which imposed restrictions or rejected pro-development proposals rather than favoring the industry. Exit polls cited by CBS News found about 76% of voters said they would oppose a data center being built in their community, and concerns over rising electricity bills (a Goldman Sachs analysis cited a projected roughly 6% national increase) were a major driver of that sentiment. Crypto, AI, and online-gambling interests spent more than $377 million trying to influence the midterms, but Wall Street still reacted with a broad, multi-trillion-dollar sell-off in AI stocks following the results, reflecting fears that political backlash could slow the roughly $2.5 trillion in planned AI data-center investment tied to state and local races.

Chip Maker Marvell Agrees to Buy Lumentum in $80 Billion Deal
Marvell Technology agreed on November 2, 2026 to acquire optical components maker Lumentum in an all-stock deal valued at about $80 billion in equity and $115 billion in enterprise value including debt, with Lumentum shareholders receiving 4.5 Marvell shares for each Lumentum share. The exchange ratio implies roughly $1,132 per Lumentum share, a premium of about 23.9% over the companies' unaffected closing prices as of October 28, 2026. Marvell shareholders will own about 67% of the combined company and Lumentum holders about 33%, with the companies citing demand for optical connectivity in AI data centers and projecting a combined $550 billion addressable market and more than $1 billion in annual run-rate cost synergies; the deal is expected to close in the first half of 2028.

Dow Rebounds After Friday’s Selloff as Oil Slides
The Dow Jones Industrial Average surged 1,104 points (2.15%) to close at 52,389.75 on Monday, November 9, 2026, rebounding from the prior Friday's selloff, while the S&P 500 gained 2.35% and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 2.74%. The rally was fueled by a sharp drop in oil prices, with Brent crude falling 5.85% to $92.57 a barrel and WTI crude sliding 4.38% to $86.85, after comments from President Trump suggested further U.S. military strikes against Iran were unlikely amid talks to end the conflict. Semiconductor stocks led the tech rebound, with the iShares Semiconductor ETF up more than 8%, AMD rising about 13%, and Broadcom climbing nearly 9%, though Nvidia fell roughly 4% following news its board would hire outside counsel to review CEO Jensen Huang's ties to a woman identified by U.S. intelligence as a Chinese spy; industrials also outperformed, rising about 4.2%.

This Bond Measure Says Stocks Are Cheap. Don’t Believe It.
Treasury yields climbing toward 5% in 2026 have narrowed the gap between stock earnings yields and bond yields to levels last seen in the late 1990s, with the S&P 500's equity risk premium compressing to roughly zero or turning negative by some measures, according to Bloomberg-sourced data reported by Fortune on September 29, 2026. As of November 13, 2026, the S&P 500 traded around 18.5 times forward earnings — below its five- and ten-year averages near 19-20x — even as the 10-year Treasury yield pushed to roughly 5.15%-5.24% that week, a combination some analysts read as a signal stocks are undervalued relative to bonds. Critics of that read argue the comparison is flawed because it pits a nominal Treasury yield against a real earnings yield (corporate profits grow with inflation, bond coupons don't), so a fairer gauge would stack earnings yield against inflation-protected Treasury (TIPS) yields, a gap that has also narrowed sharply and no longer signals cheap stocks. Ray Dalio separately warned in October 2026 that the earnings-growth cushion stocks have used to absorb rising bond yields is shrinking, leaving less protection if free cash flow weakens.

Kaz Resources, a tungsten mining company with a project in Kazakhstan, is planning to sell shares to the public through a direct U.S. listing as early as next year in a deal valued at roughly $2 billion, giving Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump indirect ownership stakes.
