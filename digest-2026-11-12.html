Who Is Colette Kress? Meet Nvidia’s New Interim CEO
Nvidia's board announced on November 12, 2026 that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and board member effective immediately, with Executive Vice President and CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO while retaining her finance role. Kress, who joined Nvidia in 2013 after nearly 25 years in finance at Cisco and Microsoft, has been on dozens of earnings calls alongside Huang and is a known figure to investors and the board, which is now searching for a permanent successor among internal and external candidates. Huang's exit followed reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman, Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Nvidia partner Megaspeed who is suspected of being a Chinese intelligence asset amid an ongoing probe into whether Megaspeed helped Chinese firms obtain roughly $4.8 billion worth of restricted Nvidia chips. Huang has since been summoned to testify before Congress over the matter.

Nvidia’s Meteoric Rise, in One Chart
Nvidia became the first company in history to reach a $5 trillion market valuation on October 29, 2025, after shares climbed more than 3% that day, having already vaulted from $1 trillion in June 2023 to $2 trillion in February 2024, $3 trillion in June 2024, and $4 trillion in July 2025; its valuation sat near just $400 billion before ChatGPT's late-2022 debut ignited the AI boom. Shares pushed past $5 trillion again on April 24, 2026, closing at a record $208.27 (up 4.3% that day) on an AI- and semiconductor-sector rally, with the stock up more than 14-fold since the end of 2022. CEO Jensen Huang said in late 2025 that the company had secured over $500 billion in orders through 2026, calling it unprecedented revenue visibility for a technology company. More recently, the stock has pulled back sharply, falling to around $162.85 by November 13, 2026 — roughly 32% below its October 6, 2026 closing record of $239.24 — after Huang's resignation knocked Nvidia's market cap below $4 trillion.

Google Faces Renewed Risk of EU Penalty Over AdSense Platform
On November 12, 2026, EU Advocate General Juliane Kokott advised the Court of Justice of the European Union to overturn the General Court's September 2024 ruling that had annulled Google's €1.49 billion ($1.7 billion) AdSense fine, saying judges had overlooked evidence that Google's AdSense for Search contracts could restrict competition. The European Commission originally imposed the penalty in March 2019, accusing Google of using exclusivity and restrictive clauses in AdSense contracts to block rivals, with Alphabet held jointly liable for part of it. Kokott recommended the case be sent back to the General Court for a fresh ruling rather than simply reinstating the original fine, suggesting any renewed penalty should be somewhat lower since one year of the alleged infringement is no longer contested. Her opinion is non-binding and the full court has not yet set a date for its final decision.

Foxconn Stays Bullish After Robust Quarter, Cautious About AI Data-Center Outlook
Foxconn (Hon Hai) reported a record Q3 2026 net profit of NT$77.5 billion (about US$2.35 billion) on November 12, 2026, up 34% year-over-year, while revenue jumped roughly 47% to NT$3.02 trillion, beating an LSEG consensus estimate of NT$69.6 billion; cloud and networking products, driven by AI servers, made up 54% of revenue versus 51% the prior quarter. Chairman Young Liu called AI servers the group's most important growth engine and said AI server rack shipments are expected to more than double for full-year 2026 and climb by a high double-digit percentage again in 2027. Despite the bullish AI-server outlook, management struck a cautious note on broader data-center and component dynamics, warning that chip, memory and key component supply will likely stay tight through 2027 and that general-purpose server growth would be only moderate, with thinner gross margins limiting how much of the AI boom flows to the bottom line.

Companies Dole Out Mega Paydays and Luxury Trips to Compete for AI Talent
AI startups and tech giants are offering unprecedented compensation to lock down scarce technical talent: U.S. startup Serval is hiring 100 engineers with $30,000 signing bonuses plus $10,000 in travel stipends, while venture-backed startups now offer median base salaries around $200,000 for software engineers, up 25% from 2022, with some recent computer-science graduates fielding offers over $300,000. Series D-stage startups are granting employee stock packages worth $2 million to $4 million to secure key hires, and OpenAI CEO Sam Altman has said Meta dangled signing bonuses as high as $100 million to poach top researchers. The fiercest competition centers on a pool of fewer than 1,000 elite AI research scientists capable of building today's most advanced large language models, even as broader tech hiring cools.

Hoping to Sell Your Home in the Spring? It’s Not Looking Good
This is the data most likely underlying the WSJ piece, since the headline refers to a spring selling season that's "not looking good" given current conditions reported in mid-November 2026. Existing-home sales fell 5.5% in October 2026 to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million — the slowest pace since August 2010 — and were down 8.8% from a year earlier, according to the National Association of Realtors' November 12 report; the median sale price still rose 0.4% year-over-year to $416,700, marking 40 consecutive months of annual price gains, while unsold inventory climbed to a 5.1-month supply. The slump is tied to 30-year mortgage rates that stayed above 7% through October and reversed higher to roughly 7.50% by mid-November, pricing out buyers and pushing the income needed to afford a median-priced home to about $103,000. Looking ahead to the spring 2027 selling season, Goldman Sachs economists project existing-home sales could fall to just 3.9 million for the year — the lowest annual total in decades — and a survey of real-estate agents found about half expect sales to keep declining and nearly 60% expect prices to fall if rates stay above 7%. Agents also reported that 39% of prospective sellers have already delayed listing their homes, underscoring why forecasters see little relief for sellers heading into next spring.

Cravath Employee Is Charged With Insider Trading Using Confidential Deal Information
The SEC and federal prosecutors in the Eastern District of New York charged Chetram "Tony" Balli, a 54-year-old document processing specialist who had worked at Cravath, Swaine & Moore since 1989, with insider trading on November 12, 2026. Prosecutors allege that from May 2025 to October 2026, Balli used his access to confidential deal filings—he helped prepare and submit SEC EDGAR filings for the firm—to trade ahead of at least eight corporate announcements, including Paramount Skydance's acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, E2open Parent Holdings' $2 billion investment, Universal Health Services, Pacira Biosciences, and Forge Global Holdings, netting roughly $55,000-$70,000 in illicit profits. The FBI arrested Balli at his home in Hollis, Queens; he faces SEC civil charges for violating Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 of the Securities Exchange Act, plus criminal charges carrying a maximum of 20 years in prison, and Cravath confirmed he is no longer employed there.

Home Sales in October Plummet to 16-Year Low
Existing-home sales in the U.S. fell 5.5% in October to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million units, the slowest pace since August 2010, the National Association of Realtors reported on November 12, 2026. Once Upon a Farm Slashes Outlook Following ‘Tractor Wheels’ Recall
Once Upon a Farm cut its fiscal 2026 outlook to net sales of $290 million–$300 million, down from a prior forecast of $327 million–$335 million, and now expects an adjusted EBITDA loss of $4.5 million to $7 million versus previously guided positive EBITDA of $3 million to $4.5 million, after a voluntary recall of its Tractor Wheels toddler snack bars. Once Upon a Farm estimates recall-related costs of up to $30 million, covering written-off inventory, legal fees, and product returns, and does not expect the bars to return to shelves until sometime in fiscal 2027 pending possible design, formulation, and packaging changes. Shares fell in after-hours trading following the November 2026 earnings disclosure.

LIV Golf’s Castoffs Have Only One Option—the Tour They Tried to Kill
I now have solid, concrete details confirmed across multiple non-WSJ outlets. LIV Golf's collapse was confirmed November 5, 2026, in a New Jersey bankruptcy court, after Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund—which had poured more than $5 billion into the five-year-old league since 2022—pulled funding in April 2026 and a backup $300 million "LIV 2.0" lifeline from BC Partners fell through on November 4, 2026, triggering liquidation proceedings that began November 9, 2026. With contracts voided, former LIV players seeking a way back to competitive golf face the PGA Tour's strict reinstatement rules: a one-year suspension from their last LIV event and a requirement to earn status through Q-School, the Korn Ferry Tour, or the DP World Tour rather than any automatic return. A handful of exceptions exist—Brooks Koepka was reinstated for 2026 via the Tour's temporary Returning Member Program for major champions away at least two years, and Patrick Reed and Eugenio Chacarra earned 2027 PGA Tour cards through DP World Tour performances—but most LIV defectors, including stars like Jon Rahm, Bryson DeChambeau, and Cameron Smith, now have no path except competing their way back up through the sport's traditional qualifying ranks.
