- **Even Personal-Finance Devotees Struggle With Roth Conversions and IRS Rules** — Roth IRA conversions carry no IRS income limit and no annual dollar cap, but several technical rules trip up even experienced savers: the pro-rata rule requires aggregating all traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRA balances (using the December 31 year-end value, not the conversion-date balance) to determine what portion of a conversion is taxable, so a $100,000 combined balance with $80,000 pre-tax would make 80% of a backdoor conversion taxable. A 2026 conversion must leave the IRA by December 31, 2026 to count for that tax year, with no April filing extension, and estimated tax on the converted amount is generally due by the next quarterly deadline (mid-January) or a penalty applies retroactive to the prior April. Since 2018, conversions can no longer be undone through recharacterization, and savers who pay the conversion tax out of the IRA funds themselves (rather than from outside cash) reduce what actually reaches the Roth while still owing tax on the full converted amount. _Sources: <https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/20/roth-conversions-have-no-april-extension-to-count-for-2026-the-money-has-to-leave-the-ira-by-december-31/>, <https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/19/shell-convert-70000-to-a-roth-in-december-and-plan-to-pay-the-tax-in-april-the-payment-is-due-jan-15-and-without-one-form-the-irs-will-charge-a-penalty-back-to-last-april/>, <https://fedlaws.org/ira-pro-rata-rule-and-aggregation-for-backdoor-roth-examples-and-fixes/>_
