Nvidia Tells Partners to Pause Shipments to Southeast Asia
Nvidia has directed manufacturing partners, including Dell and Super Micro, to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia as of November 26, 2026, while it reviews whether regional partners meet U.S. export requirements. The move follows a Commerce Department rule change this month that closed a loophole allowing Chinese firms to remotely access Nvidia chips via data centers in those countries — a channel the Institute for Progress estimates supplied about 24% of China's AI computing power. The case of Singapore-based Megaspeed, which became the region's top Nvidia buyer before being accused of smuggling chips to China, has heightened scrutiny. It's unclear how long the halt will last, but it threatens Nvidia's business in a region where its Singapore entity alone booked $104 billion in 2025 revenue.

A Game-Changing Move Just Gave America the Edge Over China in AI
I have enough confirmed from NBC News, Fortune, and the Commerce Department rule details to write the brief. The U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on November 16, 2026, closing a loophole that let Chinese companies remotely rent access to advanced Nvidia AI chips housed in overseas data centers rather than importing them directly. The rule now requires export licenses for shipping advanced chips to Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, and Brazil, and bars foreign data centers from giving remote access to companies headquartered in China, Russia, or Iran once they supply more than 12 million units of "aggregate total processing performance" to those customers. Investigators flagged a Malaysian firm, A201, which had built data centers specifically to funnel compute to a Chinese tech company, as a key target of the crackdown. The rule was developed jointly with the Treasury Department and National Security Council and follows reporting that remote cloud access to chips abroad had become a significant source of China's AI computing capacity.

The Mad Dash for Memory Chips—and the Pain It’s Causing Holiday Shoppers
Memory chip prices have surged dramatically in 2026 as AI data center demand outstrips supply, with a 16Gb DDR5 chip's contract price climbing from $8.70 in October 2025 to roughly $48 by September 2026, and global DRAM revenue jumping 369% year-over-year in the third quarter to nearly $200 billion. Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron have sold out their high-bandwidth memory (HBM) production to AI customers months in advance—SK hynix has said its DRAM and HBM output is sold out through 2029—squeezing supply for the general-purpose memory used in laptops, phones, and other consumer devices. Analysts at Gartner and TrendForce project PC prices rising roughly 17% and conventional DRAM contract prices climbing 90-95% in early 2026 as manufacturers pass costs to shoppers this holiday season, with Costco among retailers warning customers of higher electronics prices. Industry watchers expect the shortage to persist into 2027 or later, since new fabrication capacity takes years to come online.

Jensen Huang’s Vision for the New Nvidia Is a Lot Like the Old One
I have enough to write the brief with concrete facts on the reinstatement, stock move, and strategic continuity. Jensen Huang was reinstated as Nvidia's president, CEO, and board member on November 23, 2026, just 11 days after resigning on November 12 amid a scandal involving extramarital affairs, one with a subordinate and another with a woman U.S. officials reportedly believe may have been spying for China; Nvidia shares jumped more than 31% on the reinstatement news. The board said it received input from senior U.S. government officials, including President Trump, who said he personally pressed the board to bring Huang back, and the board concluded his return posed no national security risk. At a company-wide all-hands meeting on November 24, Huang apologized to employees, said he had been in daily therapy, and stressed that Nvidia's underlying strategy—full-stack dominance from GPUs to the CUDA software ecosystem, now extending into PC chips, networking, and the "Vera Rubin" roadmap—remains intact even as rivals like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft push custom AI silicon. Huang has argued these in-house chip efforts rarely reach the scale needed to challenge Nvidia's ecosystem lock-in.

Asian Markets Mostly Higher as Sentiment Stabilizes
Asian equity markets steadied on November 26, 2026 after an 11-session slide, with South Korea's KOSPI jumping 3%, Japan's Nikkei 225 adding 1.5%, Australia's S&P/ASX 200 up 0.7%, Hong Kong's Hang Seng gaining 0.5%, and Taiwan's benchmark rising 0.8%. The rebound was led by technology shares and helped by easing concerns over U.S. trade tensions, though worries about the sustainability of the AI investment boom kept risk appetite in check. Despite the day's gains, MSCI's broadest Asia-Pacific index excluding Japan was still down about 8.2% for the month of November, with analysts cautioning the recovery could be short-lived.

Pensions Trusted by Millions of Industrial Workers Make a Surprising Comeback
Multiemployer pension plans, which cover millions of unionized industrial and trucking workers, ended 2025 with an aggregate funded ratio of 103%, the highest level recorded in Milliman's 18-year funding study and up from 97% at the end of 2024, driven by strong equity returns and continued employer contributions. About 69% of the 1,180 plans tracked (812 plans) are now funded at 100% or more, and 90% are at least 80% funded, a sharp turnaround from the funding crisis that hit plans like the Teamsters' Central States Pension Fund, which required a roughly $36 billion federal bailout to project solvency through 2051. Separately, corporate single-employer pensions have also rebounded, with Milliman's index showing the 100 largest U.S. corporate plans funded at 112.2% in August 2026, up from just 77% in 2012 and the highest level since 2001, prompting companies such as IBM, JBS Foods and Northwell Health to reopen or launch new defined-benefit pension plans to attract workers.

U.S. Stock Futures Higher as AI Trade Steadies for Thanksgiving Break
US stock futures rose Wednesday evening into Thursday, with S&P 500 contracts up about 0.7%, Nasdaq-100 futures up roughly 0.6%, and Dow futures gaining around 277 points, as markets headed into the close of a Thanksgiving-shortened trading week. The move follows a volatile stretch for AI-related stocks: Nvidia jumped as much as 31% earlier in the week after Jensen Huang's reinstatement as CEO, before a subsequent selloff briefly pushed the Nasdaq 100 into bear-market territory. Traders are weighing doubts about debt-funded AI spending against expectations of further Federal Reserve rate hikes after core PCE inflation hit 3.3%, and despite the rebound, major U.S. indexes remain on pace for November losses of roughly 3% to 7.3%, with the Nasdaq Composite posting the steepest monthly decline.

Asian Stocks Rise as Investors Shrug Off U.S. Tariff Plan
Asian equity markets rallied on November 26, 2026, after the White House's new tariff proposal on 14 major trading partners—including China, Japan and the EU—came in less severe than investors had feared, with the rates not set to take effect until February or March and not stacking on top of the tariffs already imposed in July. Japan's Nikkei gained 1.6%, South Korea's Kospi rose 1.9%, and Taiwan's index added 1.4%, while MSCI's broader Asia-Pacific shares outside Japan climbed 1.5% and Chinese blue chips rose 0.6%. Analysts at JPMorgan and Capital Economics said the macro impact looks modest since the tariff review had been well telegraphed, with Capital Economics' Stephen Brown describing the plan as a "replacement rather than an escalation" of earlier duties.

Braskem Agrees to Plan Restructuring $10.9 Billion in Debt
Braskem's board approved pursuing an out-of-court "extrajudicial reorganization" to restructure $10.9 billion in unsecured financial debt, filing the petition in Brazilian court on August 24, 2026, after securing support from creditors holding 39.6% of the affected claims. Under Brazilian law, the Brazilian petrochemical producer now has 90 days from the filing date to win approval from the required majority of creditors so the updated plan binds 100% of the subject claims. The restructuring aims to extend debt maturities and build a sustainable long-term capital structure, with options including shareholder liquidity support and possible debt-to-equity conversion, while the company says operations and payments to suppliers and customers continue unaffected.
